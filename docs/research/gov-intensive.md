# gov-intensive

## Summary
The U.S. government's intensive language schools converge on a remarkably consistent formula, and its core is not a secret method — it is dosage, accountability, and needs-based design. FSI trains diplomats to ILR 3/3 ("general professional proficiency," roughly upper-B2/C1) in Spanish in 24-30 weeks of full-time study: roughly 600-750 classroom hours in groups of no more than 4-6 students with all-native-speaker instructors, PLUS an expected 2-3+ hours of independent study every day — so the true total is on the order of 1,000-1,100 hours, and the published estimates explicitly assume "very good or better" classroom-learning aptitude. DLIFLC runs an even heavier schedule (7 hours/day, 5 days/week, 2-3 hours of nightly homework, 36-64 weeks depending on language, with 1-to-3-day English-forbidden "isolation immersion" events each semester), and the FBI mostly does not train at all: it hires native and heritage speakers and gates them with an ILR-based test battery (listening, reading, translation, then twin ~45-minute Speaking Proficiency Tests in English and the target language). In all three systems, high-stakes ILR testing — with pay, assignments, and tenure attached — is a deliberate engine of motivation and washback.

On pedagogy, the most valuable primary source is Jackson & Kaplan (FSI, 1999), which is candid that FSI long ago abandoned pure audio-lingualism (the method behind the famous 1960s "FSI Basic Course" tapes) in favor of eclectic, proficiency-driven teaching: early explicit grammar overview, focus on form, authentic materials, job-relevant tasks, and huge amounts of interactive speaking — while still keeping occasional pattern drills because "the automatization of basic grammatical structures and communicative routines is essential for efficient learning." That position sits in the middle of a genuinely contested literature: the Pennsylvania Project (1970) found audio-lingual instruction no better (arguably worse) than cognitive approaches, and Wong & VanPatten (2003) argue mechanical drills are "neither necessary nor beneficial," while DeKeyser's skill-acquisition research supports systematic meaningful practice for automatization. The honest reading: mechanical substitution drills have weak evidence; deliberate, meaning-bearing practice to automatize high-frequency patterns has decent theoretical and institutional support.

For a solo learner pairing an AI voice partner with self-study, the government-program features that transfer are: time-on-task density (the single factor FSI calls "crucial" — it tried shortening programs and "it has not worked"), a needs-based curriculum built around what you will actually do in the language, one-on-one interactive speaking with corrective feedback, early-and-recurring explicit grammar, and scheduled immersion "events." The features that do not transfer automatically — and must be engineered — are external accountability testing (FSI/DLI students face career-changing exams; a solo learner should buy real ACTFL OPI/OPIc or DELE/SIELE test dates to recreate washback), cross-session institutional memory (FSI teaching teams track each learner; ChatGPT sessions start fresh, so curriculum state and error logs must live in external documents), peer and multiparty conversation (FSI's Lesson 10 says free-wheeling multiparty conversation is the hardest skill and one-on-one practice does not fully build it), and trustworthy accent/level evaluation (FSI explicitly targets intelligibility, not native accent, and uses trained human raters; AI self-assessment of pronunciation and ILR level is unvalidated).

## Findings
### FSI classifies Spanish as Category I and budgets 24-30 weeks of full-time training (roughly 600-750 classroom hours; Spanish specifically 24 weeks in the current chart, 23-24 weeks / 575-600 hours in FSI's own 1999 paper) to reach ILR S-3/R-3 'general professional proficiency.'
The target, S-3/R-3, is roughly ACTFL Superior / upper-B2-to-C1 — professional working proficiency, not colloquial 'fluency.' FSI's published figures have drifted slightly over time (575-600 hours in Jackson & Kaplan 1999; 600-750 hours on recent state.gov charts, with French at 30 weeks and Spanish at 24), so treat 'about 600-750 class hours' as the honest range. Critically, the FSI table's own footnote assumes a native English speaker with no prior knowledge and 'very good or better aptitude for classroom learning of foreign languages,' and notes 'less skilled language learners typically take longer.' About two-thirds of FSI students meet or exceed their proficiency goal.
Sources: Jackson & Kaplan 1999, 'Lessons learned from fifty years of theory and practice in government language teaching' (full text): https://archive.org/details/fsi-lessons-learned-gurt-1999-07; Language Testing International, 'How long does it take?': https://www.languagetesting.com/how-long-does-it-take; State Dept Foreign Language Training page (archived): https://2017-2021.state.gov/foreign-language-training/; Atlas & Boots FSI ranking reproduction: https://www.atlasandboots.com/travel-blog/foreign-service-institute-language-difficulty/

### The classroom-hour figures understate total effort: FSI expects 'at least four class hours a day—usually more—for five days a week, plus three or more additional hours a day of independent study.'
That quote is from FSI's own staff (Jackson & Kaplan, Lesson 4). A 2024 firsthand FSO account describes five hours of class daily plus 'a couple of hours in daily independent study,' in classrooms fitting 3-6 people. So the real cost of Spanish 3/3 from zero is roughly 1,000-1,100 total hours (about a 1:0.5-0.75 class-to-homework ratio), not the 600 often quoted. FSI also reports it has tried to shorten programs 'and it has not worked.'
Sources: Jackson & Kaplan 1999: https://archive.org/details/fsi-lessons-learned-gurt-1999-07; FSO account 'Suggested Tips for Language Study at FSI' (2024): https://collectingpostcardsblog.com/2024/10/08/suggested-tips-for-language-study-at-fsi/

### FSI's structural formula is tiny classes (max 6 for cognate languages at lower levels, 4 or fewer otherwise), 100% native-speaker instructors, plus at least one team member who learned the language as an adult to serve as a learner model.
Jackson & Kaplan state class-size limits explicitly and note that occasional one-on-one work 'intensifies time on task' and provides security to experiment — but that pure tutorial training is NOT best for most learners, who benefit from collaborating with classmates. All FSI language teachers are native speakers raised in the culture; the 'successful adult learner' team member mentors students on learning itself. Recent secondary sources report classes now capped around 4.
Sources: Jackson & Kaplan 1999: https://archive.org/details/fsi-lessons-learned-gurt-1999-07; LegalClarity overview of FSI courses: https://legalclarity.org/foreign-service-language-courses-eligibility-and-resources/

### Modern FSI pedagogy is proficiency-driven eclecticism, not the 1960s audio-lingual 'FSI Basic Course': early explicit grammar overview, focus on form, authentic materials, job-relevant tasks, conversation practice, and learner-autonomy training.
FSI describes its own evolution 'from a strict diet of sentence-based pattern drills to a range of communicative activities... to a heavy emphasis on authentic or found materials and realia,' and from 'teaching the textbook' to 'helping each student find ways to learn.' Its Accelerated Personalized Training principles: students take responsibility for their learning, research the tasks they will face at post, shape their own schedules, and are taught 'how to learn' for after the course. Notably, FSI admits outcomes in the 1960s were about as good as today — suggesting intensity, small groups, and accountability matter more than method. Area/professional studies are handled by a sister school (SPAS), while language courses embed job-related and cultural content.
Sources: Jackson & Kaplan 1999: https://archive.org/details/fsi-lessons-learned-gurt-1999-07; State Dept School of Language Studies (archived): https://2009-2017.state.gov/m/fsi/sls/index.htm; ADST oral history on FSI teaching: https://adst.org/2016/12/teaching-the-foreign-service-to-speak-foreign-languages/

### FSI's ten 'lessons learned' are the closest thing to an official evidence summary from a U.S. government language school, and several directly contradict fashionable SLA positions.
Highlights: (1) adults (mean age 41 at FSI) can reach near-native functional levels; (2) aptitude varies and MLAT is FSI's best single predictor, but 'motivation, self-discipline, and power of concentration may be equally or more important'; (3) there is no one right method or syllabus, but explicit grammar instruction helps most people; (4) time on task and intensity are 'crucial'; (5-6) prior high-level language knowledge helps while weak knowledge of a related language 'may be an actual hindrance,' and prior formal study helps 'no matter how remote'; (7) automaticity matters more than SLA research admits; (8) teachers can accelerate 'readiness' via focus on form — waiting for structures to 'emerge naturally' 'just does not work' under time constraints; (9) supportive teacher relationships and frequent formative feedback are key; (10) ordinary multiparty conversation is the hardest skill.
Sources: Jackson & Kaplan 1999 (Georgetown University Round Table 1999, pub. Georgetown UP 2001): https://archive.org/details/fsi-lessons-learned-gurt-1999-07; Springer chapter 'Language Learning at the Foreign Service Institute': https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1057/9781137528506_9

### FSI's end-of-training test is a high-stakes ILR speaking + reading proficiency exam whose consequences (assignments, tenure, promotion, incentive pay) are an explicit motivation and washback engine.
Per the 2020 National Academies review, the speaking and reading tests each run about an hour; speaking historically included social conversation, a work-related exchange, and an information-gathering interview; scoring is consensus-based on comprehension, organization, grammar, vocabulary, and fluency. Language Incentive Pay runs 5% of salary for 2/2, 10% for 3/3, 15% for 4/4, and scores expire after five years below 4/4. Reforms in 2022-23 moved speaking to a scenario-based professional-meeting format with about half the weight on listening comprehension, and new rubrics emphasize 'communicative effectiveness and fluency more than grammatical and lexical precision,' dropping native-speaker references.
Sources: National Academies, 'A Principled Approach to Language Assessment: Considerations for the U.S. Foreign Service Institute' (2020): https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/25748/chapter/4; AFSA, 'Language Testing Reforms: What You Need to Know': https://afsa.org/language-testing-reforms-what-you-need-know

### DLIFLC delivers 7 hours of classroom instruction per day, five days a week, with 2-3 hours of homework nightly, over 36 weeks (Category I-II languages, including Spanish) to 64 weeks (Category IV: Arabic, Chinese, Korean, Japanese, Pashto).
DLIFLC's own About page: 'resident instruction... five days a week, seven hours per day, with two to three hours of homework each night'; 'Courses last from 36 to 64 weeks, depending on the difficulty of the language.' That makes even Spanish roughly 1,200+ classroom hours plus ~400-500 homework hours — far more than FSI's Spanish course, but DLI starts with younger enlisted students selected by the DLAB aptitude test and targets strong listening/reading for intelligence work. About 1,500 instructors teach there, 95% native speakers, organized in teaching teams. Under the Proficiency Enhancement Plan, student-faculty ratios dropped from 10:2 to 6:2 for hard languages and 8:2 for easier ones.
Sources: DLIFLC About: https://www.dliflc.edu/about/; AUSA 'DLI's language guidelines': https://www.ausa.org/articles/dlis-language-guidelines; DLIFLC General Catalog: https://www.dliflc.edu/wp-content/uploads/2016/12/GeneralCatalog_Online-Color_FY17-18.pdf

### DLIFLC's 'isolation immersion' is a formal curriculum component: one 1-day, one 2-day, and one 3-day off-post event (semesters I, II, III) conducted exclusively in the target language with English forbidden, built around simulated real-life scenarios.
DLIFLC's Immersion Language Office defines iso-immersion as 'foreign language training conducted in total isolation from the English language,' held at a dedicated facility (Ft. Ord/Weckerling), integrated into the basic programs for Arabic, Korean, Chinese, Persian Farsi, Russian, and Spanish. FSI's parallel finding (Jackson & Kaplan, Lesson 4): immersion experiences pay off most in 'morale, motivation, perception of skill, and stamina,' and 'appear to have the greatest payoff above the S-2 level' — FSI even argues in-country immersion is most effective for learners already at higher proficiency.
Sources: DLIFLC Immersion Language Office: https://www.dliflc.edu/academics/immersion-language-office/; DLIFLC Dialog article on iso-immersion: https://www.dliflc.edu/ojs/dialog/article/download/15/9; Jackson & Kaplan 1999: https://archive.org/details/fsi-lessons-learned-gurt-1999-07

### DLIFLC graduation is gated by the DLPT (listening + reading) and an OPI (speaking): the historical floor was L2/R2/S1+, and DoD directed raising it to 2+/2+ (with speaking 1+) by FY2022 — accountability pressure that drove class-size, faculty, and curriculum reforms.
The '2+/2+ by 2022' initiative (directive issued Oct 1, 2016) rested on 'three pillars: prepared students, trained and ready faculty, and an improved and flexible curriculum,' with decreased student-teacher ratios and higher-level instruction training. Washback is designed to be positive: because the DLPT5 is a proficiency test built on authentic materials rather than a course-content test, DLIFLC's own guidance tells students to prepare through authentic news/broadcast exposure, not memorization. Graduates' scores also drive Foreign Language Proficiency Pay, so testing continues to shape behavior after graduation.
Sources: DLIFLC 'Getting to 2+/2+ by 2022': https://www.dliflc.edu/getting-to-222-by-2022/; DTIC 'Student Achievement Indicators at DLIFLC': https://apps.dtic.mil/sti/trecms/pdf/AD1150437.pdf; Wikipedia, Defense Language Proficiency Tests: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Defense_Language_Proficiency_Tests

### The FBI mostly buys language capability rather than building it: it hires native/heritage speakers as language analysts and contract linguists and gates them with an ILR-based Foreign Language Test Battery rather than running an intensive language school.
The battery comprises listening comprehension, reading comprehension, and translation tests; applicants who pass then take Speaking Proficiency Tests — structured telephone/live interviews of about 45 minutes each, one in English and one in the foreign language, ILR-scored (the FBI's 2012 ILR presentation documents this, including an integrated Dialect+MSA protocol for Arabic). Contract linguists need qualifying scores around ILR 2+. Testing serves three purposes: incentive-pay eligibility, pre/post-training measurement, and hiring/selection. Public documentation of FBI-run language *training* is thin: a translation/interpretation training center for IC linguists was set up in 2010, and DOJ OIG audits (2004, 2005) drove 18 reforms in hiring, training, QC, and backlog management — but nothing like FSI/DLI classroom pipelines for teaching agents a language from scratch.
Sources: FBI Language Testing and Assessment Unit, 'Testing Arabic Speaking Proficiency at the FBI' (ECOLT 2012): https://www.govtilr.org/Publications/FBI2012.pdf; FBI 'Linguistics: An Inside Look' brochure: https://manoa.hawaii.edu/careercenter/wp-content/uploads/2020/04/Linguist.pdf; FBI biz page, Independent Contract Linguist: https://biz.fbi.gov/fbi-foreign-language-program/independent-contract-linguist-an-inside-look; DOJ OIG audit of FBI foreign language translation program: https://oig.justice.gov/sites/default/files/legacy/reports/FBI/a0425/final.pdf; FBI 'Language Services' fact sheet: https://ucr.fbi.gov/ten-years-after-the-fbi-since-9-11/just-the-facts-1/language-services.pdf

### The audio-lingual method itself failed its biggest empirical test: the Pennsylvania Project (Smith, 1970) found audio-lingual classes no better — on several measures worse — than 'cognitive' instruction using L1 and explicit grammar.
The state-funded project spanned 58 secondary schools and 104 teachers comparing audio-lingual ('functional skills') against grammar-oriented approaches, and found no long-term superiority for ALM in speaking or listening. Combined with the theoretical collapse of its behaviorist foundations (Chomsky's critique of Skinner; Rivers 1964), this ended ALM's dominance. The famous free 'FSI Spanish Basic Course' tapes are artifacts of that era — FSI itself no longer teaches that way, which matters for self-learners tempted to treat those materials as 'the FSI method.'
Sources: EBSCO Research Starter, Audio-lingual Method: https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/language-and-linguistics/audio-lingual-method; Smith, P.D. 1970, 'A Comparison of the Cognitive and Audiolingual Approaches to Foreign Language Instruction: The Pennsylvania Project' (discussed in above and in Savignon 1991): https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.2307/3587463

### What survived of audio-lingualism is contested: mechanical drills are condemned by influential SLA researchers, yet FSI explicitly retains occasional pattern practice for automatization, and skill-acquisition theory supports systematic *meaningful* practice.
Wong & VanPatten (2003, Foreign Language Annals) conclude mechanical 'form-only' drills are 'neither necessary nor beneficial' for acquisition and should be discarded. But Jackson & Kaplan report: 'many of our students desire occasional pattern practice. Pattern practice—drill—is a technique that continues to be useful for FSI learners, when used in concert with the various communicative, experiential, and task-based approaches... the automatization of basic grammatical structures and communicative routines is essential for efficient learning' (citing McLaughlin's controlled-to-automatic processing account). DeKeyser's skill acquisition theory similarly backs deliberate practice that proceduralizes explicit knowledge — while distancing itself from 'mechanical, mindless drills.' Recent work on 'communicative drills' (form-focused but meaning-bearing) tries to square this circle. Practical upshot: drill only where responses require processing meaning.
Sources: Wong & VanPatten 2003, 'The Evidence is IN: Drills are OUT': https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1944-9720.2003.tb02123.x; Jackson & Kaplan 1999, Lesson 7: https://archive.org/details/fsi-lessons-learned-gurt-1999-07; DeKeyser, 'Automatization, Skill Acquisition, and Practice in SLA': https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/9781405198431.wbeal0067; Serfaty, 'Communicative Drills: The Impact of Communicative Intent on Oral Proficiency' (TESOL Quarterly): https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/tesq.70155

### Independent research partially corroborates the government programs' bet on intensity: with total hours held constant, concentrated instruction outperformed the same hours spread thin in Serrano & Muñoz's EFL studies — though the distribution-of-practice literature overall is mixed.
Serrano & Muñoz (2007, System) found 'concentrating the hours of English instruction in shorter periods of time is more beneficial... than distributing them in many months,' and follow-up studies (Serrano 2011, Language Learning) broadly supported intensive formats for adults. This aligns with FSI Lesson 4 (time on task and intensity 'appear crucial'). Caveat: cognitive-psychology spacing research (e.g., Rohrer) favors distributed practice for long-term retention of discrete material, and a 2022 state-of-the-art review finds distribution effects in L2 are complex and moderated by what is being learned. The defensible synthesis: high weekly density (many hours/week) beats a trickle, while within-skill review should still be spaced.
Sources: Serrano & Muñoz 2007, 'Same hours, different time distribution: Any difference in EFL?': https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0346251X07000425; Serrano 2011, 'The Time Factor in EFL Classroom Practice' (Language Learning): https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1467-9922.2010.00591.x; SSLLT state-of-the-art review of distribution-of-practice effects on L2: https://pressto.amu.edu.pl/index.php/ssllt/article/view/35602; Rohrer 2015 review on distributed practice: http://uweb.cas.usf.edu/~drohrer/pdfs/Rohrer2015EDPR.pdf

### FSI treats native accent as a non-goal — intelligibility is the criterion — and treats free-wheeling multiparty conversation as the single hardest skill, harder than professional discussion or interviews.
Jackson & Kaplan: 'Most adults are not good at eliminating accents... Native accent is typically not a practical criterion for success (although intelligibility is).' And Lesson 10: graduates 'unanimously claim to experience the most difficulty' with ordinary conversation in multiparty settings — following rapid topic shifts, turn-taking speed, background noise, producing unplanned speech — to the point that officers in Europe report interlocutors switching to English if they can't keep up. FSI built specific activities (e.g., Kaplan's 'reception game') to train this. The 2022-23 FSI test reforms likewise shifted rubric weight toward communicative effectiveness and fluency over grammatical precision.
Sources: Jackson & Kaplan 1999, Lessons 1 and 10: https://archive.org/details/fsi-lessons-learned-gurt-1999-07; Kaplan 1997, 'Learning to converse in a foreign language: The reception game' (Simulation and Gaming 28(2)), cited therein; AFSA on test reforms: https://afsa.org/language-testing-reforms-what-you-need-know

### FSI's attrition observation — a 'critical mass' effect — implies that a learner who stops below solid S-3 will likely lose the skill, while one at S-3+ maintains or improves.
Jackson & Kaplan: an individual leaving training 'with a borderline professional proficiency (or lower) is very likely to experience attrition. An individual with a strong professional proficiency (S-3 or S-3+) will maintain or improve proficiency, and with advanced professional proficiency... will almost certainly continue to improve.' For a rusty AP-4 learner (~15 years dormant), this both explains the decay and argues for pushing past borderline 3 rather than stopping at 'good enough.' FSI also notes prior formal study helps 'no matter how remote it was' — relearning is faster than learning.
Sources: Jackson & Kaplan 1999, Conclusions and Lesson 6: https://archive.org/details/fsi-lessons-learned-gurt-1999-07

### Explicit early grammar plus 'focus on form' is a consistent government-program position precisely because unmonitored communicative practice risks automatizing errors — a direct warning for false-intermediate learners.
Jackson & Kaplan: 'If there is insufficient early focus on form... learners may, indeed, risk automatizing ingrained errors (see Higgs and Clifford 1982),' and letting structures 'emerge naturally... just does not work' under time constraints; instructed consciousness-raising can make learners 'ready' to acquire forms sooner. Ray Clifford (DLI) is cited for the claim that 'frequent and constructive formative feedback' on language use is crucial scaffolding for adults. A returning learner with fossilized high-school Spanish errors is the exact profile Higgs & Clifford described ('terminal 2/2+ students').
Sources: Jackson & Kaplan 1999, Lessons 3, 8, 9: https://archive.org/details/fsi-lessons-learned-gurt-1999-07; Higgs & Clifford 1982, 'The push toward communication' (ACTFL Foreign Language Education Series 13), cited therein

## Design implications
- Budget honestly against the FSI benchmark: Spanish 3/3 from zero costs ~600-750 class hours plus ~300-450 self-study hours in selected, high-aptitude, full-time learners. A 500-hour plan for a rusty AP-4 learner (plausibly re-testable at ILR 1+/2 after reactivation) targeting solid S-3 is realistic only if hours are dense and well-aimed; treat 500 hours as tight, not generous.
- Copy FSI's in-class/out-of-class ratio rather than making the AI session the whole program: each 1-hour GPT-Live conversation session should sit inside a ~2.5-3 hour daily block (session + 1.5-2 h independent reading, listening to authentic audio, vocabulary/spaced repetition, and next-session prep). Roughly 150-200 of the 500 hours should be live conversation; the rest independent study — that mirrors FSI's structure, not Duolingo's.
- Density matters more than calendar length: FSI/DLI run 25-35 class hours/week and FSI found shortened-but-diluted programs fail; Serrano & Muñoz found concentrated hours beat the same hours spread thin. Aim for a minimum viable intensity (e.g., 10-15+ h/week in sustained pushes, with planned 'surge' periods), not 3-4 h/week for years.
- Engineer external accountability testing, because it is the one pillar an AI cannot provide: book real, paid, human-rated milestones on a fixed calendar — an ACTFL OPI or OPIc via Language Testing International (ILR-correlated), and/or DELE/SIELE — at start (baseline), midpoint, and end. Government programs deliberately use high-stakes tests to drive effort and give washback; self-quizzing inside ChatGPT has no comparable force.
- Build the external memory layer the programs get from teaching teams: since every ChatGPT session starts fresh, maintain a versioned curriculum-state document (current estimated level, syllabus week, active grammar targets, rolling error log, vocabulary due for recycling, session scripts) and paste a compact digest at the start of every session. This replicates FSI's per-learner tracking and Clifford's 'frequent formative feedback' loop; without it the AI cannot deliver cumulative correction.
- Spend AI session time only on what FSI uses class time for: interactive speaking with focus-on-form correction, scenario/task rehearsal (the post-2022 FSI speaking test is literally a simulated professional meeting — have the AI run meeting, interview, negotiation, and social-reception scenarios), targeted communicative drills, and debriefs of authentic materials read/listened to beforehand. Do reading, flashcards, and passive listening outside the session.
- Use drills the way FSI does, not the way the 1960s FSI tapes do: occasional, short pattern practice for automatizing high-frequency structures (subjunctive triggers, past-tense contrasts, clitic pronouns), designed as communicative drills where the answer depends on meaning — not mechanical substitution. The AI is genuinely good at generating unlimited such items on demand.
- Front-load an explicit grammar audit: FSI gives early grammatical overviews and warns (Higgs & Clifford) that communicative practice without focus on form automatizes errors — the core risk for a false-intermediate. Devote early sessions to a systematic error hunt (have the AI probe known AP-Spanish weak points: ser/estar, preterite/imperfect, subjunctive, por/para), log fossilized errors, and make each a recurring focus-on-form target.
- Deliberately train the hardest skill — multiparty, unplanned conversation — which one-on-one AI chat under-trains: use fast native podcasts/TV for multi-speaker listening, ask the AI to role-play multiple characters with abrupt topic shifts and interruptions, and supplement with periodic human group conversation (tutors, meetups, travel). FSI's Lesson 10 says this, not grammar, is where trained professionals still fail.
- Schedule DLIFLC-style 'isolation immersion' punctuation events: a Spanish-only day each month early on, escalating to 2-3 day English-forbidden blocks (or a Mexico trip) once past ~S-2, where the payoff is greatest per both DLI design and FSI experience. Use them for morale, stamina, and self-assessment, not new material.
- Set an accent goal of intelligibility, not nativeness, per explicit FSI policy — and don't trust the AI as sole judge: use it for perception/production practice (minimal pairs, shadowing, read-alouds) but validate pronunciation periodically with a human tutor or recording review, since real-time voice models' phonetic-diagnosis reliability is unvalidated.
- Plan the endgame around the 'critical mass' attrition finding: design the 500 hours to overshoot borderline 3 (or accept maintenance mode indefinitely), and build a post-program maintenance schedule (weekly conversation + ongoing reading) because skills parked below solid S-3 decay.
- Replicate the 'adult learner model' role FSI staffs deliberately: have part of the plan (or occasional AI sessions in English) function as meta-learning consultation — reviewing what's working, adjusting methods — since FSI found learning-how-to-learn support materially affects outcomes.

## Open questions
- No published evidence exists on whether a consumer real-time AI voice model can substitute for FSI/DLI native-instructor conversation hours: dialect consistency, error-correction accuracy in speech mode, willingness to let the learner struggle (vs. accommodating), and phonetic feedback reliability are all unvalidated.
- How many of the learner's original hours 'count': there is no good public data on relearning curves for false-intermediate adults (FSI's internal 'conversion training' report, Thain & Jackson, is unpublished), so the true distance from rusty AP-4 to S-3 is a guess until a baseline OPI is taken.
- How much of FSI/DLI success is selection rather than method: students are screened (MLAT/DLAB), career-motivated, and full-time; FSI itself notes 1960s methods produced similar outcomes, implying method effects are second-order — but the counterfactual is untestable from public data.
- Optimal hour distribution for a part-time learner: government evidence confounds intensity with full-time immersion in study; Serrano & Muñoz support concentration but spacing research favors distribution for retention — where the crossover lies for a 10-15 h/week self-learner is unresolved.
- Current FSI Spanish-section daily practice is thinly documented publicly (most pedagogical detail dates to the 1999 Jackson & Kaplan paper plus officer anecdotes and archived state.gov pages; state.gov currently blocks archiving/fetching); an FOIA'd or insider syllabus would sharpen the model.
- FBI in-house language training for special agents (beyond testing and the 2010 IC translation training center) is essentially undocumented publicly — it remains unclear whether the Bureau ever trains agents to professional proficiency from scratch or only leverages existing speakers.
- Whether ILR-style external tests (ACTFL OPI, SIELE) create the same productive washback for a self-directed learner as career-stakes tests do for FSOs and military linguists — the motivational literature suggests stakes matter, but self-imposed stakes are weaker.
- The precise evidentiary status of communicative drills remains contested: Wong & VanPatten's position vs. FSI practice vs. DeKeyser's skill-acquisition account have not been adjudicated by a definitive meta-analysis specific to oral-proficiency outcomes in adults.
