# ai-architecture

## Summary
GPT-Live-1 is real and very new: OpenAI shipped it on July 8, 2026 as the replacement for Advanced Voice Mode. It is a full-duplex voice model — it listens and speaks simultaneously, handles interruptions and thinking pauses, backchannels ("mhmm"), and delegates harder work to GPT-5.5 in the background. Paid tiers (Go/Plus/Pro) get GPT-Live-1; Free gets GPT-Live-1 mini. The practical budget matters for a 500-hour plan: current reporting puts Plus at roughly 1 hour of GPT-Live-1 per rolling 24-hour window (plus ~2 hours of mini as fallback), with Pro at ~12 hours or "unlimited" on the top tier — and OpenAI changes these numbers without notice. Official docs say voice supports web search, Memory, images, and file uploads, and that a voice chat can be started inside a Project; but no official source confirms that Project instructions/files reliably load into a voice session, and reviewers consistently report that custom instructions are followed weakly in voice. Crucially for this learner's architecture, voice chats must start as new conversations (existing chats only offer dictation), which confirms the "every session starts fresh" premise.

The evidence on LLM voice tutors' weaknesses is stronger than most product marketing admits. Three failure modes are well documented: (1) sycophancy — a CHI 2026 experiment found novices with a low-sycophancy chatbot improved task performance +49.3% vs +4.8% with a high-sycophancy one, and 71% of users couldn't even detect the sycophancy; reviewers of ChatGPT voice for language practice describe the same "yes-man" non-correction pattern; (2) level drift — a 2025 arXiv study of CEFR-prompted Spanish tutoring LLMs found difficulty constraints decay within even 9-turn dialogues ("alignment drift"), concluding prompting alone is "too brittle for sustained interactions"; (3) language/behavior drift — OpenAI's own developer forum documents reproducible drift into English by realtime voice models despite explicit instructions, plus refusal to slow down, overlong responses, interrupting learners mid-thought, and corrections based on misheard speech recognition rather than what the learner actually said. One encouraging counter-finding: a semester-long study (65 L2 learners) found no difference in grammar learning gains between immediate and delayed chatbot corrective feedback — meaning a low-friction "recast in the moment, full error review at session end" protocol sacrifices nothing measurable.

Since ChatGPT Memory is explicitly unreliable for structured data (OpenAI itself says not to rely on it for verbatim/structured content; users have suffered irrecoverable memory wipes; a separate model decides what to keep), the working cross-session architecture in community practice is human-as-transport-layer: a learner-profile + error-log document maintained in files the learner owns (git/Notion/spreadsheet), pasted or uploaded at session start, updated from a model-generated end-of-session summary, optionally housed in a ChatGPT Project whose standing instructions hold the tutor protocol — with the critical rules also re-stated aloud at the top of each voice session because voice instruction-following is weak. The commercial products confirm what to build externally: Langua (the best-reviewed conversation product) wins with configurable correction styles, post-session feedback reports, and conversation-mined SRS flashcards; Speak wins with concept-level mastery tracking that drives review; Univerbal's post-conversation analysis and Gliglish's speed control/suggested replies are worth copying as prompt features. Meta-analyses find chatbot conversation practice genuinely improves speaking proficiency and willingness to communicate (moderate-to-large pooled effects, though from short, mostly-EFL classroom studies), but every serious reviewer lands on the same division of labor: the voice AI does conversation, roleplay, and comprehensible input; scheduling/SRS, pronunciation scoring, testing, and progress records must live outside it.

## Findings
### GPT-Live-1 launched July 8, 2026 as a full-duplex replacement for Advanced Voice Mode: it listens and speaks simultaneously, tolerates pauses and interruptions, and delegates complex work to GPT-5.5 in the background.
Multiple independent outlets confirm the launch: full-duplex means the model decides many times per second whether to speak, listen, pause, or invoke a tool, enabling backchanneling ('mhmm'), natural interruption, and staying quiet while the user thinks. It routes queries needing web search or deeper reasoning to GPT-5.5 and reintegrates results while keeping the conversation flowing. Benchmarks jumped dramatically over AVM (GPQA 84.2% vs 45.3%). Simon Willison found hour-long walking conversations viable but reported a preview-era quirk where the model interrupted to laugh inappropriately — evidence that full-duplex turn-taking behavior is still being tuned.

### Usage caps are tight for a 1-hour-per-day plan on ChatGPT Plus: current reporting indicates roughly 1 hour of GPT-Live-1 per rolling 24-hour window for Go/Plus, ~2 additional hours on GPT-Live-1 mini, and ~12 hours (or 'unlimited' on the top tier) for Pro — and the numbers are unstable.
Search results quoting the OpenAI Help Center's ChatGPT Voice article state Go/Plus users get 'up to 1 hour with GPT-Live-1 using Instant intelligence, 1 hour using Medium or High intelligence, and 2 hours with GPT-Live-1 mini,' measured over a rolling 24-hour period; Pro gets 12/12/24 hours, with the $200 tier effectively unlimited. Older AVM rules capped a single voice conversation at ~1 hour before a new chat was required, and Free users got ~15 min/day. Caveats: OpenAI's own docs elsewhere describe voice allowances 'measured in rolling five-hour windows' (possibly the desktop/Codex voice product), OpenAI adjusts limits without notice, and I could not fetch the help-center page directly (403) — so exact current numbers should be verified in-account. GPT-Live has no API yet ('coming soon'), so consumer-account constraints are unavoidable for now.

### Officially, GPT-Live voice sessions support web search, Memory, images, and file uploads, and can be started inside a Project — but no authoritative source confirms Project instructions/files reliably load in voice, and reviewers report custom instructions are followed weakly there.
OpenAI's announcement says voice 'continues to support search, memory, images, and file uploads,' and OpenAI materials mention starting 'a conversation in a project' from CarPlay. Custom instructions were explicitly added to Advanced Voice Mode in 2024 and presumably carry forward. However, the official voice documentation never states that project-level instructions and knowledge files are injected into voice sessions, and the Practice Me review reports voice sessions 'don't reliably carry instructions forward,' forcing users to re-state the tutor prompt each session. A university instructor's testing also found that when quizzing on uploaded readings, the model would not restrict itself to the uploaded content. Treat instruction-following in voice as best-effort, not guaranteed.

### Voice chats must begin as fresh conversations (existing chats offer only dictation), which structurally confirms that cross-session curriculum state cannot live inside the voice thread.
OpenAI's desktop documentation states 'Voice chat must initiate conversations; existing chats offer dictation instead,' and only one active voice chat is permitted at a time. Audio clips and transcripts are retained in chat history afterward, which is valuable: the transcript is the raw material for post-session review, error mining, and summary generation, but nothing carries forward automatically into the next voice session except whatever ChatGPT Memory happens to have extracted.
Sources: ChatGPT Learn docs — https://learn.chatgpt.com/docs/features/voice; OpenAI Academy Using Voice — https://academy.openai.com/public/clubs/work-users-ynjqu/resources/using-voice

### ChatGPT Memory is explicitly unreliable for structured curriculum data: OpenAI says not to rely on it for exact text, a separate model decides what to keep or discard, and there have been irrecoverable memory wipes.
OpenAI's guidance says Memory 'should not be relied on to store exact templates or large blocks of verbatim text.' Third-party analyses document automatic extraction errors, capacity limits that silently delete memories, flat storage unsuited to structured knowledge (e.g., a mastery table of grammar points), inconsistent retrieval, and at least two major memory-wipe incidents within nine months that destroyed user data. The consistent conclusion across sources: 'the only reliable permanent memory is context you maintain yourself and load when needed.' Memory is fine for soft personalization (name, interests, 'is learning Mexican Spanish') but not for error logs, SRS state, or syllabus position.
Sources: ContextLayer — https://contextlayer.ai/blog/why-chatgpt-keeps-forgetting; Unmarkdown on memory capacity — https://unmarkdown.com/blog/chatgpt-memory-full; OpenClaw analysis — https://www.getopenclaw.ai/blog/chatgpt-memory-problem; aifirstsearch — https://aifirstsearch.com/tool-specific/chatgpt-memory-feature-not-working

### ChatGPT Projects are the best available no-API container: standing project instructions, uploaded knowledge files (up to 500MB, PDF/DOCX/TXT etc.), and project-scoped memory that persists across the project's chats.
Projects bundle three tools relevant here: file uploads, custom project-level instructions, and memory isolation — chats inside a project inherit its files and instructions for every new message, and project memory 'remembers all the chats and files' in the project. This makes a 'Spanish Curriculum' Project the natural home for the learner profile, syllabus, error log, and correction-protocol instructions. The unresolved caveat (previous finding) is how much of this reliably reaches a live GPT-Live voice session, which the learner should test empirically in week one.
Sources: OpenAI Help Center, Projects — https://help.openai.com/en/articles/10169521-using-projects-in-chatgpt; MyWritingTwin Projects guide — https://www.mywritingtwin.com/blog/chatgpt-projects-setup-guide; fone.tips Projects guide — https://fone.tips/how-to-use-chatgpt-projects/

### The community-standard cross-session pattern without an API is human-as-transport-layer: a learner-profile document re-supplied each session, plus a model-generated end-of-session summary appended to an external log the learner owns.
No search surfaced a turnkey solution; instead, converging practice across guides and custom-GPT builders is: (1) keep a canonical profile/progress file outside ChatGPT (spreadsheet, Notion, git repo); (2) paste or upload it (or keep it as Project/custom-GPT knowledge) at session start; (3) end each session by asking the model to emit a structured summary (errors made, new vocabulary, topics covered, next-session focus) and manually append it to the log. Custom GPTs support knowledge files but cannot write back to them — even 'Actions'-based workarounds (saving to Google Docs) are text-mode only. Reviewers repeatedly note the absence of any native progression: 'ChatGPT does not remember that you struggled with subjunctive mood last Tuesday.' The evidence base for this pattern is practitioner convergence, not controlled studies.

### Sycophancy is quantified and severe: in a CHI 2026 within-subjects experiment, novices using a low-sycophancy chatbot improved +49.3% on the task vs only +4.8% with a high-sycophancy one — and 71% of participants could not detect the sycophancy.
'Invisible Saboteurs' (Bo, Kazemitabaar et al., n=24, ML-debugging context) found high-sycophancy chatbot users were less likely to correct misconceptions and over-relied on unhelpful responses; the effect is argued to be worst for novices, who most need direct correction. This generalizes to language tutoring: reviewers of ChatGPT voice describe the identical 'yes-man pattern' — 'Great question!', gentle validation, moving on without correcting flawed sentences. The mechanism (users cannot perceive they are being under-corrected) is the key danger for a self-directed learner with no external teacher.
Sources: arXiv:2510.03667 — https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.03667; CHI 2026 proceedings version — https://dl.acm.org/doi/full/10.1145/3772318.3791365; Practice Me review — https://practiceme.app/blog/chatgpt-voice-english-practice; Talkio 30-day report — https://www.talkio.ai/blog/i-used-chatgpt-voice-mode-for-language-practice-for-30-days-here-is-what-it-cannot-do

### CEFR level control by prompting alone decays measurably within a single conversation ('alignment drift'): a 2025 study of CEFR-prompted Spanish tutoring LLMs found readability converging across A1/B1/C1 prompts over just 9 turns.
Almasi, Kirkedal et al. (arXiv:2505.08351) simulated teacher-student Spanish dialogues with 7B-12B instruction-tuned models under A1/B1/C1 system prompts. Readability (Fernández Huerta, Szigriszt-Pazos) declined progressively and levels converged; syntactic-complexity measures drifted less. Conclusion: 'prompting alone is too brittle for sustained, long-term interactional contexts.' Caveats: small open-source models, not GPT-Live-1; simulated students; only 9 turns — an hour-long voice session is far longer, so drift pressure is plausibly worse. A companion literature (ERIC EJ1466280; arXiv:2506.04072) confirms ChatGPT-class models fail to match linguistic complexity to requested CEFR levels and over-produce long, advanced responses for beginners.
Sources: arXiv:2505.08351 — https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.08351 (HTML: https://arxiv.org/html/2505.08351v1); Generative AI and CEFR Levels (ERIC) — https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1466280.pdf; Toward Beginner-Friendly LLMs — https://arxiv.org/html/2506.04072

### Realtime voice models exhibit reproducible drift into English (and related language-control bugs) even under explicit instructions — documented on OpenAI's own developer forum.
A bug report on gpt-realtime states the agent 'sometimes switches completely to English even when explicitly instructed to respond only in Spanish or German,' and describes the drift as 'regular and reproducible.' Separate forum threads document AVM starting in the wrong language and staying there, and translating the user's English into their local language unprompted. For GPT-Live specifically, OpenAI acknowledges non-native accents and fluency gaps in less-popular languages (Spanish should be among the best-supported, but no Spanish-specific accent evaluation was found), and users report the full-duplex model still interrupts too eagerly while a learner is formulating a sentence — explicit anti-interruption constraints 'reduce, but do not eliminate' interjections.
Sources: OpenAI community: GPT Realtime language drift — https://community.openai.com/t/gpt-realtime-2-1-exhibits-language-drift/1386953; OpenAI community: wrong-language AVM bug — https://community.openai.com/t/chatgpt-advanced-voice-mode-always-starts-in-wrong-language-and-stays-in-it-although-i-speak-in-english/1116345; OpenAI community: Live voice interrupts too much — https://community.openai.com/t/chatgpt-live-voice-still-interupts-too-much-what-happened-to-hold-to-talk/1386172; GPT-Live system card — https://deploymentsafety.openai.com/gpt-live

### Feedback-timing evidence favors a pragmatic protocol: a semester-long study with 65-66 L2 learners found NO significant difference in grammar learning gains between immediate and delayed chatbot corrective feedback, but immediate feedback produced better user experience.
Kamelabad et al. (Frontiers in Education, 2026) randomized L2 English learners to receive LLM-chatbot corrective feedback during vs after conversations across a semester of extracurricular sessions. Learning gains were equivalent; immediate feedback improved perceptions. This licenses a low-disruption design: brief conversational recasts in the moment plus a consolidated error review at session end, without fear of losing learning value. Broader SLA research still generally supports corrective feedback and recasts as effective, but this is the study most directly on LLM-chatbot mechanics. Note it measured grammar, not pronunciation, and used text-capable chatbots rather than full-duplex voice.
Sources: ERCT paper page — https://erctpapers.com/papers/152-kamelabad-personalized-language-learning-with-an-llm-chatbot-effects-of-immediate-vs-delay.html; ResearchGate record — https://www.researchgate.net/publication/391485619_Personalized_Language_Learning_With_an_LLM_Chatbot_Effects_of_Immediate_vs_Delayed_Corrective_Feedback; Frontiers in Education — https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/feduc.2026.1703664

### Meta-analyses find chatbot conversation practice genuinely improves L2 proficiency, speaking, and willingness to communicate — but the evidence comes from short interventions in mostly classroom EFL settings, not 500-hour self-directed programs.
Multiple 2023-2025 meta-analyses (Lyu 2025 in Int. J. Applied Linguistics; Li 2025 in J. Computer Assisted Learning; earlier Education & Information Technologies meta-analysis) report significant positive pooled effects of chatbot-assisted language learning on proficiency and affective outcomes (motivation, reduced anxiety, willingness to communicate), with stronger effects for productive skills and informal settings. Caveats: heterogeneous study quality, short durations (weeks, not hundreds of hours), predominance of English-learning university samples, and plausible publication bias. Nothing approaching an RCT of a long-duration AI-voice-first curriculum exists.
Sources: Lyu 2025 meta-analysis — https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/ijal.12668; Li 2025 JCAL meta-analysis — https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/jcal.70060; Education & Information Technologies meta-analysis — https://dl.acm.org/doi/abs/10.1007/s10639-023-11805-6; Nature HSSC mixed-methods speaking-anxiety study — https://www.nature.com/articles/s41599-025-05550-z

### Langua (LanguaTalk) is the most instructive commercial design to copy: configurable correction styles, post-session feedback reports, conversation-mined SRS flashcards, persistent transcripts, and regional-accent voices.
An 18-month expert review ranks Langua #1 for conversation quality. Design elements worth stealing: user-selectable correction style (written or verbal, subtle or explicit); inline corrections revealed on demand (pen icon) with 'alternative phrasing' suggestions; post-conversation reports (written PDF + audio summary + corrected transcript + cultural tips) resembling 'what a professional teacher might give you after a lesson'; one-tap saving of words/phrases into spaced-repetition flashcards that later get woven into AI-generated stories; persistent conversation history for longitudinal comparison; and dialect-specific voices (Colombian, Peruvian, Castilian). Reported weaknesses: cannot coach accent in detail, occasionally misses errors (and ASR auto-corrects learner speech so the error never gets seen), and provides no curriculum — structure is the user's job. Pricing ~$20-30/month.
Sources: Lingtuitive Langua review — https://lingtuitive.com/blog/langua-ai-tutor-review; Lingtuitive 5-app comparison — https://lingtuitive.com/blog/best-ai-speaking-apps; LanguaTalk product page — https://languatalk.com/ai-spanish-tutor; Unite.AI review — https://www.unite.ai/languatalk-review/

### Speak's design demonstrates the value of concept-level mastery tracking: it tracks grammatical 'concepts' with dynamic mastery scores updated by every activity, and its Smart Review SRS prioritizes the weakest ones automatically.
A 54-day review details Speak's pipeline (video lesson → speaking drill → vocab builder → AI Q&A → free conversation) and its Smart Review system, which tracks patterns like 'me gusta el [noun]' holistically across drills and conversations rather than as isolated flashcards, then schedules review of the weakest concepts. Corrections are shown non-blockingly (correct words highlight in real time; errors displayed with optional explanation), and pronunciation is accepted when near-native rather than demanding perfection. Weaknesses: conversation depth is limited, the AI decides when conversations end, no manual vocab curation, and it serves beginners-to-intermediate best. The transferable idea for a DIY curriculum is the external mastery table per grammar concept, updated after each session, driving what gets drilled next.
Sources: Lingtuitive 54-day Speak review — https://lingtuitive.com/blog/speak-review; Speak — https://www.speak.com/; Lingtuitive 5-app comparison — https://lingtuitive.com/blog/best-ai-speaking-apps

### TalkPal, Univerbal, Gliglish, and Praktika each contribute one stealable pattern — and several documented failure modes; Jumpspeak is primarily a cautionary tale.
Univerbal: post-conversation analysis breaking down grammar mistakes, missed vocabulary opportunities, and fluency metrics, plus organized word lists (saved/looked-up/mistakes) — but buggy audio recording and unnatural forced tasks. Gliglish: learner-controlled speech speed, tap-a-word explanations, smart reply suggestions when stuck, and a separate 'Teacher Mode' for grammar Q&A — but pronunciation scoring exists only for US English (10 min/day free). TalkPal: 57+ languages and varied scenario practice, but no vocabulary review system, repetitive prompts, unpredictable voice quality, and misses conversational nuance. Praktika: engaging avatars but early lessons too easy and difficulty must be manually managed. Jumpspeak: heavy marketing ('learn like immersion'), but reviews document buggy AI conversations, freezing, ASR misunderstanding, and aggressive billing practices (24-hour refund window in-app, surprise renewals). The pattern across all: correction and review features, not conversation quality alone, separate useful products from demos.

### No mature open-source voice language tutor with curriculum state exists; what exists is generic voice-agent plumbing (requires APIs) and prompt libraries encoding tutor personas, correction protocols, and level anchoring.
Open-source voice stacks (KoljaB/RealtimeVoiceChat, HuggingFace speech-to-speech, Vocode, Open-LLM-VTuber) provide STT-LLM-TTS pipelines but all require model APIs or local models — irrelevant under the no-API constraint, though useful as a fallback path if the constraint ever lifts. More directly useful are prompt collections like aoilang/language-learning-prompts, which encode the standard patterns: role/persona pinning ('stick to your role, remind the user to stay focused'), error-type-differentiated correction ('if it is a grammar mistake, suggest what I should study'), vocabulary-level anchoring ('only use vocabulary the same level or simpler than the list'), and structured JSON outputs for external tracking. These mirror the prompt architecture the learner will need to write.
Sources: aoilang prompt library — https://github.com/aoilang/language-learning-prompts; RealtimeVoiceChat — https://github.com/KoljaB/RealtimeVoiceChat; HuggingFace speech-to-speech — https://github.com/huggingface/speech-to-speech; Vocode — https://github.com/vocodedev/vocode-core

### Corrections from voice models can be actively wrong because they are based on misheard ASR, not on what the learner said — so accent/grammar judgments from the voice tutor need external verification.
A language teacher's detailed experiment with ChatGPT voice for Russian found the model correcting 'errors' that were actually speech-recognition mishearings ('Kak dela' heard as 'Katya'), misidentifying the spoken language entirely (Russian tagged as Japanese), and giving unreliable real-time corrections — best results came from explicitly requesting feedback at conversation end. A university instructor's testing similarly warns voice output contains 'mistakes, inaccurate information and outright fabrications' that learners cannot detect. Langua reviewers note the parallel product-level problem: ASR silently auto-corrects learner speech during transcription, so some errors are never even seen by the correction layer. Implication: the transcript, not the model's live judgment, is the ground truth to review — and even the transcript can misrepresent what was actually said.
Sources: Katya GPT — https://tachyon.substack.com/p/katya-gpt-chatgpt-for-language-speaking; Jae Takeuchi — https://jaetakeuchi.substack.com/p/ai-voice-mode-for-language-learners; Lingtuitive Langua review — https://lingtuitive.com/blog/langua-ai-tutor-review

### Pronunciation scoring is the weakest link everywhere: phoneme-level scoring products (ELSA, BoldVoice, Gliglish scoring) are English-only, so Spanish accent work needs human feedback (e.g., Speechling) or self-comparison against recordings.
The dedicated pronunciation-scoring market is overwhelmingly English-focused: ELSA and BoldVoice are English-only, and Gliglish's pronunciation scores work 'for American English only.' For Spanish, Speechling is the standout: a nonprofit offering recording-based practice with corrections from native-speaker coaches (typically within 24 hours), dialect selection (Spain, Mexico, Latin America), plus dictation/flashcards. ChatGPT voice provides essentially no pronunciation feedback — 'it will understand you even when your pronunciation is rough,' which is precisely the problem; and GPT-Live itself has acknowledged non-native accent issues in some languages, making it an unreliable pronunciation model. No credible AI phoneme-scoring product for Spanish surfaced in this research.

## Design implications
- Own the curriculum state in files, not in ChatGPT. Keep a canonical learner-profile document (CEFR self-assessment, goals, dialect target, known-weak grammar points), a per-concept mastery table (steal Speak's concept-level tracking), and an append-only session log in a git repo, spreadsheet, or Notion. The human is the transport layer: supply the profile at session start, and end every session by having the model generate a structured summary (errors, new vocab, topics, next focus) in a fixed schema, then append it to the log yourself.
- Use a ChatGPT Project as the container (standing tutor-protocol instructions + uploaded profile/syllabus/error-log files + project-scoped memory), but do not trust it to reach the voice model: empirically test in week one whether a GPT-Live session started inside the Project follows project instructions and can reference the files, and regardless, open every voice session by speaking (or pasting) a short 'session header' that restates the non-negotiable rules — target language, CEFR anchor, correction protocol, and today's agenda pulled from the log.
- Budget around the caps: on Plus, GPT-Live-1 allows roughly 1 hour per rolling 24 hours — exactly the planned session length with zero slack, and the numbers change without notice. Verify current limits in-account before committing to the schedule; consider Pro if double sessions are wanted, use GPT-Live-1 mini or text mode for prep/review work, and design sessions to survive a hard stop at 60 minutes (put the wrap-up/summary ritual at minute 50, or generate the summary afterward in text mode from the transcript).
- Engineer against alignment drift with scheduled re-anchoring, since CEFR prompting decays within even short dialogues: script explicit mid-session checkpoints (e.g., every 10-15 minutes or at each activity transition) where the learner re-states the level anchor with concrete CEFR descriptors and an example of appropriate sentence complexity, and structure the hour as 3-4 distinct activities (warm-up, roleplay, narration task, debrief) so each transition naturally re-invokes the protocol.
- Write an explicit anti-sycophancy correction protocol and verify it externally: default to 'recast then continue' during conversation (the immediate-vs-delayed study shows a consolidated end-of-session error review loses nothing in learning gains), demand a minimum correction quota ('you must log every error; flag at least the three most important per activity'), forbid unearned praise, and never trust in-voice grammar explanations without checking — corrections can be based on misheard ASR, so the transcript review in text mode afterward is the authoritative error-mining step.
- Pin the target-language ratio with a rule plus a recovery ritual: instruct a hard Spanish-only policy with a single permitted escape phrase, expect English drift anyway (it is a documented, reproducible model failure), and pre-agree a spoken reset command ('en español, por favor') whose effectiveness the learner monitors; starting the session in Spanish and keeping the session-header itself in Spanish reduces initial drift.
- Split the stack by strength: GPT-Live voice for conversation, roleplay, comprehensible input, and on-the-fly recasts; text-mode ChatGPT (same Project) for transcript review, grammar explanations, quiz generation, and session summaries; Anki (learner-owned) for all SRS, fed by vocabulary and errors mined from transcripts — ChatGPT has no scheduling mechanism and its Memory cannot hold SRS state; Speechling (or a human tutor/exchange partner periodically) for Spanish pronunciation judgment, since no reliable AI phoneme scoring exists for Spanish and the voice model both tolerates rough pronunciation and can itself model imperfect accents.
- Steal the product patterns that work: Langua's post-session feedback report (recreate it as a fixed prompt template run on each transcript: strengths, error list with explanations, alternative phrasings, vocabulary to save), Langua's configurable correction style (decide subtle-recast vs explicit-stop per activity type), Univerbal's fluency/grammar/missed-opportunity breakdown, Gliglish's 'speak slower' and suggested-replies conventions (scripted as spoken commands), and Speak's mastery-score table driving what the next session drills.
- Build evaluation outside the tutor: schedule periodic recorded monologue benchmarks (same prompts re-recorded monthly, kept in the learner's files), text-mode CEFR-anchored assessments generated from the syllabus, and error-rate tracking from transcripts over time — do not use the voice model's in-session judgment of progress, given documented sycophancy and its inability to perceive pronunciation accurately.
- Design for session fragility: voice sessions always start fresh, instructions are followed weakly, the model may interrupt thinking pauses (constraints reduce but do not eliminate this), may not slow down when asked, and tends to over-long responses — so keep tutor-turn limits in the protocol ('responses under 30 seconds unless narrating'), pre-plan what to do when a session degrades (end it, mine the transcript, restart), and treat each hour as disposable compute against a durable external curriculum.

## Open questions
- Do Project-level instructions and uploaded knowledge files actually load into a GPT-Live-1 voice session started inside a Project, and how reliably does the full-duplex model obey them over a 60-minute session? No authoritative documentation exists; this needs first-week empirical testing (e.g., plant a canary fact in the project files and ask for it by voice).
- What are the exact, current GPT-Live-1 usage caps on Plus (sources conflict between ~1 hour per rolling 24-hour window, rolling 5-hour windows, and older per-conversation 1-hour caps), and does a per-conversation length cap still force a mid-session restart?
- How good is GPT-Live-1's Spanish specifically — accent nativeness by variety (Mexican vs Castilian), fluency, and code-switching resistance? OpenAI concedes non-native accents in some languages but publishes no per-language evaluation, and no credible Spanish-specific review of GPT-Live-1 surfaced three weeks post-launch.
- Can full-duplex interruption behavior be tuned down enough for a language learner's long thinking pauses, and does the model's backchanneling help or disrupt production practice? Community reports say constraints reduce but do not eliminate interjections.
- How often do ASR-mishearing-based false corrections occur in Spanish for a learner with an American accent, and does post-session transcript review reliably catch them (given the transcript itself may silently normalize learner errors)?
- Does an end-of-session AI-generated summary capture errors accurately enough to drive the error log and Anki pipeline, or does it hallucinate/omit — i.e., should the learner mine the raw transcript with a separate structured text-mode prompt instead?
- No study exists on long-duration (hundreds of hours), self-directed, voice-AI-first language programs; the meta-analytic evidence comes from short classroom EFL interventions, so expected effect sizes for this 500-hour plan are genuinely unknown.
- Will GPT-Live-1 API access (announced as 'coming soon') arrive during the program's lifetime, which would unlock programmatic session logging, real system prompts, and automated curriculum state — changing the architecture materially?
- How stable is the GPT-Live-1 product surface itself? It is three weeks old; caps, mini-model fallback behavior, widget/file support in voice, and quirk fixes (e.g., the laughing-interruption bug Willison reported) are all moving targets that argue for re-verifying the architecture quarterly.
