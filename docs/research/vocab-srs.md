# vocab-srs

## Summary
The coverage research gives this learner a concrete target. In English, Nation (2006) showed that 95% coverage of everyday spoken language is reached with roughly 3,000 word families (plus proper nouns), while 98% — the level associated with comfortable, unassisted comprehension — needs 6,000–7,000 families for speech and 8,000–9,000 for written text. Spanish-specific data from Mark Davies' Corpus del Español work closely parallels this: the top 1,000 lemmas cover 87.8% of spoken Spanish, the top 2,000 cover 92.7%, and the top 3,000 cover 94.0%. So the folk claim "3,000 words ≈ 95% of speech" is approximately right for Spanish — with the caveats that Davies counts lemmas (stricter than word families, so the equivalent family count is somewhat lower), that his "oral" corpus includes interviews and broadcast speech rather than pure casual conversation, and that 95% coverage means one unknown word per 20 — enough for adequate listening comprehension (van Zeeland & Schmitt 2013) but not effortless comprehension. For a false-intermediate with an AP-4 background, much of the top 2,000–3,000 is dormant rather than absent: Bahrick's 50-year "permastore" studies of school Spanish show that a large fraction of well-learned vocabulary survives decades without rehearsal and is relearned with large savings, which argues for a diagnostic-and-reactivation phase rather than starting from zero.

On method, the evidence supports doing both deliberate and incidental learning, exactly as Nation's four-strands model prescribes (deliberate vocabulary study lives inside the ~25% "language-focused learning" strand). Deliberate paired-associate study is startlingly efficient — 30–100 new form-meaning links per hour initially — and Elgort (2011) showed with priming experiments that flashcard-learned words acquire genuinely fluent, implicit lexical representations, not just "exam knowledge." Incidental learning from input is far slower per word (meta-analytic uptake is ~9–18% of encountered unknown words, with gains plateauing after ~20 encounters; Nation's modeling says ~3–11 million tokens of reading to acquire 9,000 families) but it builds collocation, register, and fluency that cards cannot. The retrieval-practice and spacing literatures are among the most robust in cognitive psychology (testing-effect meta-analytic g ≈ 0.6; optimal review gap ≈ 5–20% of the desired retention interval), but the specific shape of the schedule matters less than folklore suggests: expanding intervals are not reliably better than equal intervals long-term — what matters is delayed, effortful, successful retrieval with feedback. Practically, that means any modern SRS is fine, and FSRS (now built into Anki) is the best-validated scheduler: benchmarked on ~700M real reviews, it predicts recall better than SM-2 for >99% of users, and its real user-facing advantage is that you set an explicit desired-retention target (~0.85–0.90 is the efficient zone; workload rises steeply above 0.90) and it schedules to hit it with roughly 20–30% fewer reviews.

On card design, the research contradicts several pieces of community folklore. L1-translation pairs are not inferior to "immersion-style" cards: Laufer & Shmueli (1997) found words glossed in L1 were retained better than L2-glossed ones, and words presented in isolation or minimal sentence context were remembered as well as or better than words embedded in elaborated text. Direction matters: productive (EN→ES) practice is harder but yields broader knowledge including production ability (Webb 2009), and harder-direction training shows better long-term retention (Schneider, Healy & Bourne 2002) — while recognition-only cards are cheaper and sufficient for coverage/comprehension. Cloze/sentence cards specifically are under-researched; keyword mnemonics and imagery boost initial learning but have mixed long-term results and are best reserved for stubborn words. The English→Spanish cognate relationship is a major asset: roughly half of academic spoken vocabulary and >70% of the Academic Word List are Spanish-English cognates, with genuinely dangerous false friends rare (<1% of academic words) — though cognates are much more transparent in writing than in listening/pronunciation, and everyday high-frequency conversational Spanish (tener, llevar, sacar...) is far less cognate-dense than formal registers.

For interlocking vocabulary with the AI-conversation core of the plan: the involvement-load literature (meta-analysis: Yanagisawa & Webb 2021) shows that "evaluation" — actually using a word in a generated context — is the strongest single contributor to incidental vocabulary learning, and de la Fuente (2002) found that negotiation-of-meaning with pushed output produced better productive vocabulary acquisition than input alone. This supports a two-way pipeline: pre-teach a small set of target words before a themed session and have the AI push the learner to use them (comprehension support from pre-teaching has modest but positive evidence), then mine unknown/failed words FROM the session into the SRS, where the genuine communicative need and the episodic memory of the conversation strengthen encoding. Budget-wise, deliberate vocabulary work is cheap relative to 500 hours: at ~10 new cards/day and 85–90% desired retention, expect roughly 15–25 minutes/day of SRS, i.e. ~120–180 hours across 18 months for 4,000–5,000 cards — but most of that should be treated as a parallel background process, not carved out of conversation-session time.

## Findings
### For English, ~3,000 word families (plus proper nouns) give ≈95% coverage of everyday spoken language, while 98% coverage — the threshold for comfortable unassisted comprehension — requires 6,000–7,000 families for speech and 8,000–9,000 for writing (Nation 2006).
Nation trialled fourteen 1,000-family BNC lists against written and spoken corpora. For spoken texts (movies, talk-back radio, family conversation) 3,000 families + proper nouns exceeded 95% coverage and 6,000–7,000 families were needed for 98%. He also reviews Hu & Nation (2000): at 95% coverage only a minority of L2 learners achieved adequate comprehension; regression suggested 98% (1 unknown word in 50) is needed for most learners. Note these are word families (courage/encourage/discouragement = 1 family), a more generous counting unit than lemmas.
Sources: Nation (2006), How Large a Vocabulary Is Needed for Reading and Listening?, Canadian Modern Language Review — https://www.lextutor.ca/cover/papers/nation_2006.pdf; https://utppublishing.com/doi/10.3138/cmlr.63.1.59

### Spanish-specific corpus data (Davies): the top 1,000 lemmas cover 87.8% of spoken Spanish, top 2,000 cover 92.7%, and top 3,000 cover 94.0% — so '3,000 ≈ 95% of speech' is approximately correct for Spanish, with caveats.
From the corpus behind the Routledge Frequency Dictionary of Spanish (built from Davies' 100M-word Corpus del Español; ~20M-word 1900s subcorpus balanced across oral, fiction, non-fiction, Spain + Latin America). Written coverage is lower: 3,000 lemmas = 88.2% (non-fiction) and 89.6% (fiction). Davies counts lemmas with parts of speech separated (pintar, pintura, pintor are three lemmas), a stricter unit than Nation's families, yet Spanish coverage still matches or exceeds English at each level. Caveat: the 'oral' component includes interviews/transcribed broadcast speech, not purely casual conversation, and the corpus is decades old. Davies' full 20k-lemma frequency list is freely downloadable.
Sources: Davies, Vocabulary Range and Text Coverage: Insights from the Forthcoming Routledge Frequency Dictionary of Spanish — https://www.lingref.com/cpp/hls/7/paper1091.pdf; A Frequency Dictionary of Spanish (Routledge) — https://www.amazon.com/Frequency-Dictionary-Spanish-Vocabulary-Dictionaries/dp/0415334292; Spanish 20k lemma list — https://www.wordfrequency.info/files/spanish/spanish_lemmas20k.txt

### The 95% vs 98% thresholds differ by skill: ~95% coverage appears adequate for listening comprehension, whereas ~98% is the standard benchmark for comfortable reading.
Van Zeeland & Schmitt (2013) tested L2 listeners at 90/95/98/100% coverage and found comprehension was similar at 90% and 95%, concluding 95% is a reasonable adequacy benchmark for listening — lower than the 98% usually cited for reading (Hu & Nation 2000; Laufer & Ravenhorst-Kalovski 2010). Adolphs & Schmitt (2003) had earlier corrected the old claim that 2,000 families cover ~99% of conversation: in CANCODE, 2,000 families gave under 95%, and ~3,000 families (or ~5,000 individual word forms) were needed for ~95–96%. Implication: for conversation-first goals, the 3,000-lemma band is the efficient core; pushing to 5,000+ mainly buys reading comfort and precision.
Sources: van Zeeland & Schmitt (2013), Applied Linguistics — https://academic.oup.com/applij/article-abstract/34/4/457/199564; Adolphs & Schmitt (2003), Lexical Coverage of Spoken Discourse, Applied Linguistics — https://academic.oup.com/applij/article-abstract/24/4/425/213596

### Deliberate flashcard-style learning is highly efficient (30–100 new form-meaning links per hour) and produces genuinely fluent, implicit lexical knowledge — not just brittle 'test knowledge.'
Nation and Webb's reviews of paired-associate learning report initial learning rates of roughly 30–100 words/hour with good retention. Elgort (2011, Language Learning) is the key study: adult learners who studied 48 pseudowords purely from word cards showed form-priming, masked repetition priming, and automatic semantic priming effects — signatures of integrated, fluently-accessed lexical representations. This directly undercuts the claim that flashcard knowledge is a separate 'explicit-only' store unusable in real time. Caveat: what is learned is the form-meaning link; collocation, register, and constraints on use still require input and use.
Sources: Elgort (2011), Deliberate Learning and Vocabulary Acquisition in a Second Language — https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1467-9922.2010.00613.x; Elgort (2011) PDF — https://www.lextutor.ca/freq/lists_download/elgort_2011.pdf

### Incidental learning from input is real but slow per word: meta-analytically, learners pick up only ~9–18% of unknown words encountered (6–17% on delayed tests), with diminishing returns after ~20 encounters.
Webb, Uchihara & Yanagisawa (2023, Language Teaching) meta-analyzed 24 studies (N=2,771): gains were similar across reading (~17%/15% immediate/delayed), listening (~15%/13%), and reading-while-listening. Uchihara, Webb & Yanagisawa (2019) found a medium correlation (r=.34) between number of encounters and learning. Nation's (2014) corpus modeling estimates ~3 million tokens of reading to meet the 9,000 most frequent families often enough to learn them (up to ~11M tokens counting sequential steps; ~1,200 hours at 150 wpm per McQuillan's interpretation). Conclusion: input alone cannot efficiently deliver a 3,000–5,000-word core on a 500-hour budget, but it is irreplaceable for depth of knowledge.
Sources: Webb, Uchihara & Yanagisawa (2023) — https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/language-teaching/article/how-effective-is-second-language-incidental-vocabulary-learning-a-metaanalysis/E38E3468FD2090B1FA3051051DE8E70C; Uchihara, Webb & Yanagisawa (2019), Language Learning — https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/lang.12343; Nation (2014), How much input do you need to learn the most frequent 9,000 words? — https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1044345.pdf

### Nation's four-strands framework assigns deliberate vocabulary study to the 'language-focused learning' strand at roughly 25% of course time — i.e., flashcards are legitimate but should be capped, with the other 75% going to meaning-focused input, meaning-focused output, and fluency development.
Nation (2007, Innovation in Language Learning and Teaching) argues a balanced course splits time equally across the four strands, with deliberate learning of high-frequency vocabulary explicitly endorsed inside the language-focused strand because of its speed. The framework is a synthesis of research-based principles rather than an experimentally validated ratio; the 25/25/25/25 split is a heuristic, not a measured optimum. For this learner it implies vocabulary SRS plus grammar study together should stay near a quarter of total study time.
Sources: Nation (2007), The Four Strands — https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.2167/illt039.0; Semantic Scholar PDF — https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/The-Four-Strands-Nation/3f2756daf68ef11b69b98e002655591a144e9ccf

### Retrieval practice (the testing effect) is one of the most robust findings in learning science: practice testing beats restudying with a mean effect size around g = 0.5–0.6, and feedback strengthens the effect.
Adesope, Trevisan & Sundararajan (2017, Review of Educational Research) meta-analyzed 272 effects from 188 experiments: overall g = 0.61 vs all comparison conditions, ~0.51 vs restudying, ~0.93 vs no activity. Providing the correct answer after retrieval attempts increases the benefit — directly applicable to SRS grading and to in-conversation quizzing with the AI providing immediate feedback. This literature is largely lab/classroom-based with mostly verbal materials, so vocabulary learning is squarely inside its domain of validity.
Sources: Adesope et al. (2017), Rethinking the Use of Tests: A Meta-Analysis of Practice Testing — https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.3102/0034654316689306

### Expanding intervals are NOT reliably better than equal intervals for long-term retention; the active ingredients are spacing itself, a delayed/effortful first retrieval, and successful recall — the precise schedule shape matters much less than SRS folklore claims.
Karpicke & Roediger (2007, JEP:LMC) found expanding schedules helped immediate tests but equal spacing won at a 2-day delay, and that delaying the first test was the key variable. Kang, Lindsey, Mozer & Pashler (2014) found no meaningful long-term difference between expanding and equal schedules over weeks. Cepeda et al. (2008, Psychological Science; N>1,350) mapped the 'temporal ridgeline': the optimal single gap is ~20–40% of a 1-week retention interval, falling to ~5–10% of a 1-year interval — long gaps beat short ones when the goal is long-term retention, and overshooting the optimum costs less than undershooting. Practical reading: any well-implemented SRS captures most of the available benefit.
Sources: Karpicke & Roediger (2007) — https://learninglab.psych.purdue.edu/downloads/2007/2007_Karpicke_Roediger_JEPLMC.pdf; Kang et al. (2014), Psychonomic Bulletin & Review — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24744260/; Cepeda et al. (2008) — https://laplab.ucsd.edu/articles/Cepeda%20et%20al%202008_psychsci.pdf

### FSRS (the modern scheduler now built into Anki) beats SM-2 on prediction accuracy for >99% of users in a ~700-million-review benchmark; its practical benefits are an explicit desired-retention dial and roughly 20–30% fewer reviews for the same retention.
FSRS models each card with three latent variables — Difficulty, Stability, Retrievability (DSR) — fitted per-user; the open benchmark (Anki revlogs from ~10,000 users, ~727M reviews) shows FSRS-6 achieves lower log loss than SM-2 for ~99.6% of users, and even default (unoptimized) FSRS parameters beat SM-2 for ~99.5%. Caveats: SM-2 was never designed to output probabilities (the benchmark bolts a conversion on top), and a research LSTM/RWKV can beat FSRS, so FSRS is 'best practical,' not theoretically optimal. The user-facing gain is scheduling to a chosen retention target (permissible 0.70–0.97; ~0.90 recommended, 0.80–0.85 acceptable for efficiency) — workload grows nonlinearly above ~0.90 (community/FSRS docs: 90%→95% roughly doubles daily reviews). The optimal-retention simulator minimizes minutes-per-item-remembered, typically landing in the 0.80s.
Sources: Benchmark of spaced repetition algorithms — https://expertium.github.io/Benchmark.html; FSRS4Anki wiki, The Optimal Retention — https://github.com/open-spaced-repetition/fsrs4anki/wiki/The-optimal-retention; FSRS4Anki tutorial — https://github.com/open-spaced-repetition/fsrs4anki/blob/main/docs/tutorial.md

### Card direction: productive (EN→ES) study is harder but produces broader gains including speaking-relevant knowledge, and harder-direction training yields better long-term retention; recognition (ES→EN) cards are cheaper and adequate for comprehension coverage.
Webb (2009, RELC Journal) had learners study word pairs receptively or productively and tested 10 aspects of knowledge: productive learning won on orthography and on productive knowledge of meaning/syntax/grammar; receptive learning won only on receptive meaning; Webb concluded that if only one format is used, productive is preferable. Schneider, Healy & Bourne (2002) found training in the harder L1→L2 direction produced worse immediate performance but better retention a week later — a desirable-difficulties result. Trade-off: production cards cost more time per item, so a mixed policy (production cards for high-frequency/high-need items, recognition for breadth) is the efficiency-rational reading, though that exact policy has not itself been experimentally validated.
Sources: Webb (2009), RELC Journal — https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0033688209343854; Schneider, Healy & Bourne (2002), Journal of Memory and Language — https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0749596X0192813X

### Sentence/context cards are not clearly better than plain L1-translation word cards for the initial form-meaning link — and L1 glosses beat L2 definitions; the cloze-card orthodoxy of the sentence-mining community is folklore, not established science.
Laufer & Shmueli (1997, RELC Journal) compared words in isolation, in a sentence, in text, and in elaborated text: L1-glossed words were retained better than L2-glossed, and isolated/single-sentence presentation was retained as well as or better than richer text contexts. Nakata's flashcard research program (2013 dissertation; later studies) finds retrieval, spacing, and feedback drive learning, with context adding some usage knowledge (collocation, register) but slowing initial mapping. A single example sentence on the card appears mildly helpful for usage without much cost. Direct experimental comparisons of cloze-deletion cards vs standard word cards in SRS at scale are essentially absent from the peer-reviewed literature — an honest evidence gap.
Sources: Laufer & Shmueli (1997), summarized in Folse (2010) — http://www2.hawaii.edu/~readfl/rfl/April2010/articles/folse.pdf; Nakata (2013), Optimising second language vocabulary learning from flashcards — https://www.academia.edu/4557533/Nakata_T_2013_Optimising_second_language_vocabulary_learning_from_flashcards_Unpublished_doctoral_dissertation_Victoria_University_of_Wellington_New_Zealand

### Keyword mnemonics and imagery reliably boost initial learning but have mixed long-term results; best used selectively for words that repeatedly fail in the SRS rather than as a default card format.
The keyword method (linking a foreign word to an L1-sounding keyword plus an image) shows strong immediate cued-recall advantages, but several studies find the advantage shrinks or reverses at delays unless pictures/keywords are re-presented, and long-term findings are explicitly mixed across the literature. Adding pictures of keyword+referent during study improved long-term retention in some work. Given a rusty intermediate's needs (mostly re-activation plus mid-frequency expansion), mnemonics are a targeted tool for 'leech' cards, not a system.
Sources: Wang & Thomas line of work, Learning by the Keyword Mnemonic: Looking for Long-Term Benefits — https://www.researchgate.net/publication/232580423_Learning_by_the_Keyword_Mnemonic_Looking_for_Long-Term_Benefits; Beaton et al., The mnemonic keyword method: bidirectional retrieval training and imaging ability — https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0959475207000357

### The cognate advantage for English speakers is large but register-skewed: about half of academic spoken vocabulary and >70% of the Academic Word List are Spanish-English cognates, while genuinely deceptive false friends are rare (<1% of academic words); estimates run to 10,000–15,000+ cognate pairs overall.
A 2025 Applied Linguistics study of the Academic Spoken Word List found ~50% cognates, >49% non-cognates, <1% false cognates; cognates covered 10.53% of the academic spoken corpus tokens. It also found cognates are more transparent orthographically than phonologically — i.e., easier to recognize in print than by ear, and pronunciation must be learned. Lubliner & Hiebert (2011) and Nash (1997) report >70% of the AWL are cognates and up to ~10,000–15,000 cognate pairs. Crucial caveat: everyday high-frequency conversational Spanish (tener, llevar, sacar, quedar...) is much less cognate-dense than academic/formal registers, so the cognate dividend pays out mostly in mid-frequency and abstract vocabulary. Classic false friends (embarazada, actual, realizar, asistir, carpeta) exist but are a small, enumerable set worth a dedicated mini-deck rather than a source of pervasive danger.
Sources: Spanish–English cognates and false cognates in academic spoken vocabulary (2025), Applied Linguistics — https://doi.org/10.1093/applin/amaf041; White Rose repository record — https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/227245/; Lubliner & Hiebert (2011) — https://www.researchgate.net/publication/233351501_An_Analysis_of_English-Spanish_Cognates_as_a_Source_of_General_Academic_Language

### Realistic SRS throughput: ~10 new cards/day is the widely-recommended sustainable rate (~80–150 mature reviews/day at default retention); SuperMemo's long-run models estimate ~200–300 items learned per year per minute of daily study; a card's lifetime review cost is on the order of a few minutes.
Wozniak's simulation work (basis of SuperMemo) puts the asymptotic acquisition rate at ~200–300 items/year per minute/day at a 10% forgetting index — e.g., 20 min/day ≈ 4,000–6,000 items/year in theory; real users report wide variance (50–2,000). Community-consensus Anki numbers: 10 new/day stabilizes around 80–120 reviews/day (roughly 15–25 min at 6–10 s/review for language cards); Anki's old default of 20/day compounds to 150–240 reviews/day and burns most people out. Gwern's synthesis offers the '5-minute rule': a card costs a few minutes of lifetime review, so memorize only items worth ≥5 minutes of future utility, and don't use SRS for material needed sooner than ~5 days. Honest budget math for the plan: 1,000 words ≈ 25–50 hours of cumulative SRS work spread over 1–2 years; a 4,000–5,000-card program ≈ 15–25 min/day ≈ 120–180 hours across 18 months. These throughput figures are engineering estimates from program documentation and large user datasets, not RCTs.
Sources: SuperMemo, Theoretical aspects of spaced repetition — https://super-memory.com/articles/theory.htm; Gwern, Spaced Repetition for Efficient Learning — https://gwern.net/spaced-repetition; Anki deck options manual — https://docs.ankiweb.net/deck-options.html; FSRS tutorial — https://github.com/open-spaced-repetition/fsrs4anki/blob/main/docs/tutorial.md

### Bahrick's very-long-term studies are directly relevant to a rusty AP-4 learner: much high-school Spanish survives 25–50 years in 'permastore,' relearning shows large savings, and very widely spaced practice (~8-week gaps) is the most durable schedule ever tested.
Bahrick (1984, JEP:General; N=733) found retention of school Spanish over 50 years was predicted by original training level and grades, with a stable permastore fraction persisting despite essentially no rehearsal. Bahrick et al. (1993, Psychological Science) trained family members on foreign vocabulary over 9 years: 13 sessions at 56-day spacing produced retention comparable to 26 sessions at 14-day spacing — half the sessions for equal long-term retention. Bahrick & Phelps (1987) showed 8-year retention of Spanish word pairs was best predicted by 30-day (vs 1-day or massed) inter-session spacing. Implications: (1) the learner's dormant vocabulary will come back far faster than new learning — plan a rapid diagnostic/reactivation pass over the top 3,000–5,000 lemmas; (2) for lifetime retention, tolerate long gaps and low apparent recall during relearning.
Sources: Bahrick (1984), Semantic memory content in permastore — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/6242406/; Bahrick, Bahrick, Bahrick & Bahrick (1993), Maintenance of Foreign Language Vocabulary and the Spacing Effect — https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1111/j.1467-9280.1993.tb00571.x; Bahrick & Phelps (1987), Retention of Spanish Vocabulary Over 8 Years — https://gwern.net/doc/psychology/spaced-repetition/1987-bahrick.pdf

### Using a word communicatively is the strongest lever on retention from meaning-focused activities: in the Involvement Load meta-analysis, 'evaluation' (using the word in a produced context) contributed most, and negotiation-of-meaning with pushed output beats input-only conditions for productive vocabulary gains.
Yanagisawa & Webb (2021, Language Learning) meta-analyzed 398 effect sizes (N=4,628): the ILH significantly predicts incidental learning (explaining 15% of immediate-posttest variance); evaluation contributed the most, need next, and search (dictionary look-up per se) contributed nothing detectable. De la Fuente (2002, SSLA) compared premodified input, negotiation without pushed output, and negotiation + pushed output in Spanish L2 oral tasks: negotiation conditions beat input-only for receptive acquisition and pushed output was superior for productive acquisition. Together these justify making the AI conversation itself the 'evaluation' stage for target words — being pushed to use pre-taught words, and having meaning negotiated in-session, is not just practice but encoding.
Sources: Yanagisawa & Webb (2021) — https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/lang.12444; de la Fuente (2002), Negotiation and oral acquisition of L2 vocabulary, SSLA — https://www.researchgate.net/publication/232005281_NEGOTIATION_AND_ORAL_ACQUISITION_OF_L2_VOCABULARY

### Pre-teaching vocabulary before a comprehension/speaking task has surprisingly thin direct evidence — it helps comprehension modestly (especially once the top ~3,000 words are known), while TBLT research warns against front-loading everything; 'just-in-time' vocabulary arising from communicative need is at least as memorable.
Reviews note very little controlled research on pre-teaching vocabulary before listening/speaking; Webb's work suggests pre-teaching can improve comprehension and processing speed. TBLT practitioners caution that heavy pre-teaching converts a task back into a presentation lesson, and recommend pre-teaching only task-essential items. Combined with the ILH findings, the evidence-consistent design is small pre-taught target sets (5–10 words) plus systematic post-hoc mining of words that caused communication breakdowns — the breakdown itself creates 'need,' the strongest naturally-occurring involvement component available in conversation.
Sources: Questions about pre-teaching vocabulary (research review) — https://adaptivelearninginelt.wordpress.com/2020/10/09/questions-about-pre-teaching-vocabulary-ptv/; Webb (2009) — https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0033688209343854; TBLT guidance — https://www.barefootteflteacher.com/p/how-to-teach-with-task-based-language

## Design implications
- Set a two-tier vocabulary target: Tier 1 = full command (recognition + production) of Davies' top ~3,000 lemmas (≈94-95% of spoken Spanish); Tier 2 = recognition-level knowledge of lemmas 3,000-5,000 plus learner-specific conversational/domain vocabulary. Use Davies' freely downloadable 20k lemma list (wordfrequency.info) as the canonical external word database the curriculum tracks against.
- Begin with a diagnostic/reactivation phase, not new learning: run the top 5,000 lemmas through a rapid self-test (e.g., batch yes/no + translation checks) to partition words into known / dormant / unknown. Bahrick's savings data predict dormant AP-level vocabulary will relearn several times faster than new words, so seed the SRS with the dormant set first for cheap early wins.
- Use Anki with FSRS enabled as the external memory layer (this is the cross-session curriculum state the fresh-context ChatGPT sessions lack). Set desired retention to 0.85-0.90, not higher — workload rises steeply and nonlinearly above 0.90 for marginal retention gain. Let FSRS's optimal-retention simulator tune it after a few thousand reviews.
- Budget vocabulary honestly: ~10 new cards/day sustained ≈ 15-25 min/day ≈ 120-180 hours over 18 months for ~4,000-5,000 cards. Decide explicitly whether this is inside or alongside the 500 conversation-hours; per Nation's four strands, deliberate vocab + grammar study together should be capped near ~25% of total study time, with the rest on input, output (the AI sessions), and fluency work.
- Card design policy grounded in evidence, not folklore: default to simple L1-translation pairs (L1 glosses outperform L2 definitions); make ES→EN recognition cards for breadth; add EN→ES production cards only for Tier-1 and conversation-mined words (harder direction = better retention and speaking-relevant knowledge); include one short example sentence for usage information but test on the word; reserve keyword-mnemonic/picture treatments for repeatedly-failed leech cards; treat cloze-only sentence cards as unproven, optional flavor.
- Exploit the cognate structure of Spanish asymmetrically: don't spend cards on transparent cognates for reading, but DO drill their pronunciation and auditory recognition (cognate transparency is orthographic more than phonological — a 4-on-AP learner will misparse cognates by ear and stress them wrong). Maintain a small dedicated false-friend deck (~100-200 items: actual, embarazada, realizar, asistir, carpeta...), since false friends are rare but high-embarrassment.
- Wire vocabulary into each AI session as a loop: (1) pre-session, export 5-10 due/target words from the SRS into the session prompt and instruct GPT-Live-1 to elicit and push use of them during the themed conversation (pushed output + 'evaluation' are the strongest retention levers); (2) in-session, ask the AI to negotiate meaning rather than instantly translate when a breakdown occurs; (3) post-session, mine every word that failed or was looked up into Anki with the conversational context noted on the card — communicative need at encoding is the strongest naturally occurring involvement factor.
- Use the AI session's opening minutes as retrieval practice, not review-reading: have the session prompt include last session's mined words and instruct the model to quiz production of them cold, with immediate feedback (testing effect g≈0.6; feedback amplifies it; a day-plus delay before first retrieval improves durability). This also substitutes for the model's lack of memory: the external prompt file IS the spaced schedule for conversational recycling.
- Recycle conversation themes at long intervals (weeks, not days), consistent with Cepeda's optimal-gap ridge (gap ≈ 5-20% of desired retention interval) and Bahrick's 8-week-spacing durability results; the external curriculum tracker should log which lemmas were actually used in which session so themes can be re-run when their vocabulary is near forgetting.
- Do not expect conversation or listening input to build the word list by itself: incidental uptake is ~10-18% of unknown encountered words and needs ~12-20+ encounters — at conversational speeds that cannot deliver thousands of words within 500 hours. The SRS carries breadth; conversation and input convert card knowledge into usable, collocationally-rich knowledge. Both strands are load-bearing and neither substitutes for the other.
- Don't over-engineer the scheduler: FSRS vs SM-2 is worth ~20-30% review efficiency, but expanding-vs-equal interval debates are second-order. The evidence-critical properties are: retrieval (not rereading), delay before first retrieval, feedback, spacing across sessions, and sustainable daily load. Any residual optimization time is better spent on card triage (suspending low-value cards) than on algorithm tweaking.

## Open questions
- There is no direct research on vocabulary acquisition through real-time AI voice conversation: does interaction with GPT-Live-1 produce the same negotiation-of-meaning and pushed-output benefits documented with human interlocutors (de la Fuente 2002), and does the model reliably notice/repair lexical errors rather than accommodating them?
- Spanish spoken-coverage figures rest on Davies' early-2000s corpus whose 'oral' component includes interviews and broadcast speech; coverage of genuinely casual Latin American conversation (the learner's Mexico use case) may require somewhat different vocabulary — no modern Spanish equivalent of van Zeeland & Schmitt's listening-threshold work was found.
- Word-family vs lemma counting makes English thresholds (6,000-7,000 families for 98% of speech) hard to map precisely onto Spanish lemma lists; nobody has published a rigorous conversion for Spanish morphology (verb families are huge; derivational families like pintar/pintura/pintor are split in Davies' counts).
- Cloze/sentence-card formats — the dominant community practice — lack head-to-head experimental comparison against plain word cards in long-running SRS use; the Laufer & Shmueli and Nakata evidence is short-horizon and pre-dates modern SRS ecosystems.
- FSRS optimizes probability of recognition-style recall at review time; whether scheduling to 0.85-0.90 recall probability also optimizes speed of lexical access in real-time speech (the thing that matters mid-conversation) is unstudied — fluency of access may need separate timed-retrieval practice.
- How large the recognition-production gap is for a rusty false-intermediate, and whether reactivated 'permastore' words return to productive availability or only receptive recognition, is not well characterized; the diagnostic phase should measure both directions separately.
- The optimal split of a fixed hour between pre-taught-word conversation practice and free conversation is unknown; ILH meta-analysis suggests evaluation-heavy tasks win for retention, but too much vocabulary steering may degrade the fluency-development value of the session (Nation's fluency strand requires using already-known language under time pressure).
- Realistic throughput numbers (10 new cards/day, seconds per review, SuperMemo's 200-300 items/year/min) come from program documentation and large observational datasets, not controlled trials; individual variance is huge (reported 50-2,000 items/year/min), so the plan should treat first-90-days telemetry from the learner's own Anki stats as the authoritative budget input.
