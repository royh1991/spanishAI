# sla-evidence

## Summary
The instructed-SLA literature supports a few robust conclusions and leaves several important questions genuinely open. The most solid findings: (1) instruction works — focused L2 instruction produces large gains over exposure alone (Norris & Ortega 2000, overall effects near d ≈ 1.0, durable on delayed posttests); (2) conversational interaction with negotiation of meaning is one of the best-evidenced drivers of acquisition (Keck et al. 2006 and Mackey & Goo 2007 both report large effects, d ≈ 0.6–1.2, that grow rather than fade on delayed posttests); (3) oral corrective feedback works (Li 2010: d = 0.64 across 33 studies; Lyster & Saito 2010: durable classroom effects, with prompts that push learners to self-correct beating recasts that merely reformulate); and (4) speaking fluency is trainable through repetition under time pressure — task repetition (4/3/2-style) produces proceduralization that transfers to new topics (de Jong & Perfetti 2011). Skill acquisition theory (DeKeyser) adds an important, experimentally supported constraint: automatization is skill-specific — comprehension practice does not transfer well to production — so speaking fluency requires large amounts of actual speaking, exactly what a conversation-centered curriculum provides.

The contested areas are real and worth taking seriously. Krashen's comprehensible-input hypothesis is regarded as unfalsifiable as stated (Gregg 1984, McLaughlin 1987), yet its core claim — that large quantities of comprehensible input are necessary — is universally accepted; the fight is over sufficiency, and Swain's immersion evidence (near-native comprehension, persistently non-native production after thousands of input hours) is the standard refutation of input-only positions. The famous "explicit beats implicit instruction" result (explicit d ≈ 1.13 vs implicit d ≈ 0.54 in Norris & Ortega; confirmed by Spada & Tomita 2010 and Goo et al. 2015) is contested on measurement grounds: most outcome measures tapped controlled, explicit knowledge, and the most recent large meta-analysis (Kang, Sok & Han 2019) found only a minor explicit–implicit difference once outcome-measure type was modeled. TBLT's headline effect (d = 0.93, Bryfonski & McKay 2019) shrank to g = 0.61 under reanalysis with tighter inclusion criteria (Xuan et al.), and Swan's 2005 critique — that strong TBLT rests on unproven hypotheses and underserves new-grammar learning in limited-exposure settings — has never been fully answered. Pronunciation instruction shows large effects on targeted features in controlled tasks but weak evidence of improving global comprehensibility in spontaneous speech (Saito & Plonsky 2019). Across the whole field, "large" effect sizes mostly come from small samples, short treatments (often under 5 hours), and researcher-made tests aligned with the treatment — Plonsky & Oswald's field-specific benchmarks (d ≈ 0.40 small, 0.70 medium, 1.00 large) are the right lens, and nothing in this literature directly validates any full 500-hour curriculum.

For a rusty post-AP learner specifically, the memory literature is unusually encouraging and unusually consistent. Bahrick's 50-year study of school-learned Spanish found that after a sharp exponential decline in the first 3–6 years, remaining knowledge stabilizes in "permastore" for decades, with the retained fraction determined mainly by original training depth and grades — an AP-4 learner sits near the top of that distribution. Recognition survives far better than recall, and receptive skills far better than productive fluency, which predicts exactly the profile this learner reports (can "get by" in Mexico but is rusty at speaking). The savings/relearning literature (de Bot & Stoessel 2000; Hansen, Umeda & McKinney 2002) shows that even items that appear completely forgotten relearn dramatically faster than new material. The practical upshot: the first phase of a 500-hour program should be aggressive reactivation through production — comprehension will return first and fastest, recall and spontaneous speech will lag, and treating oneself as a beginner would waste the single largest asset the learner has.

## Findings
### Focused L2 instruction produces large, durable gains over uninstructed exposure — this is the field's foundational meta-analytic result.
Norris & Ortega (2000) synthesized 49 experimental/quasi-experimental studies (1980–1998) and found large target-oriented gains from focused instruction (overall effects commonly cited around d ≈ 0.96 on immediate posttests), maintained on delayed posttests. Explicit instruction types outperformed implicit types (commonly cited as d ≈ 1.13 vs 0.54). Kang, Sok & Han (2019), aggregating 54 studies (N = 5,051) across 35 years, found an overall g = 1.06 (95% CI 0.84–1.29) for form-focused instruction. Caveat: these are gains on narrow, treatment-aligned tests, not broad proficiency growth.
Sources: Norris & Ortega 2000, Language Learning — https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/0023-8333.00136; ERIC abstract — https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ611436; Kang, Sok & Han 2019, Language Teaching Research — https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/1362168818776671

### The 'explicit beats implicit instruction' finding is real but contested on measurement grounds — much of the advantage may be an artifact of tests that reward explicit knowledge.
Norris & Ortega themselves flagged that ~90% of outcome measures were constrained, discrete-point tasks favoring explicit knowledge. Critics (e.g., Doughty; Taylor 2023 review) argue the meta-analyses show explicit instruction improves explicit knowledge, not the implicit knowledge underlying real-time communication; co-author Spada has acknowledged the limitation. Kang et al. (2019) found only a minor explicit–implicit difference, with outcome-measure mode a significant moderator — free-response measures shrink the explicit advantage. Spada & Tomita (2010, 41 studies) still found larger effects for explicit instruction on both simple and complex features, including some measures of spontaneous use, and Goo et al. (2015) re-confirmed the explicit advantage with an updated study pool while criticizing the original coding. This is an active, unresolved debate.
Sources: Spada & Tomita 2010, Language Learning — https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1467-9922.2010.00562.x; Goo, Granena, Yilmaz & Novella 2015 (Norris & Ortega revisited) — https://benjamins.com/catalog/sibil.48.18goo; Kang, Sok & Han 2019 — https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/1362168818776671; Taylor, 'The Justification for Explicit Grammar Instruction is Overstated', MEXTESOL Journal — https://www.mextesol.net/journal/index.php?page=journal&id_article=46438

### Krashen's input hypothesis is considered unfalsifiable as formulated, but its weak version — massive comprehensible input is necessary — is universally accepted; the consensus rejects only sufficiency.
Gregg (1984) and McLaughlin (1987) showed the core constructs (i+1, acquisition vs learning, the affective filter) cannot be independently measured, making the theory unfalsifiable; i+1 has never been empirically validated. Yet Nation (2007) notes that although many researchers criticize Krashen's theory, 'none would disagree' that meaningful comprehensible input is an important source of learning. The modern position is input-necessary-but-insufficient: neurocognitive work (e.g., Li & Jeong 2020) shows conversation-with-feedback produces greater gains and broader neural engagement than passive input. Krashen's ideas remain far more influential in online self-learning communities than in the research field itself.
Sources: Frontiers in Psychology 2025 critique — https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2025.1636777/full; Critical review of the Input Hypothesis — http://jehd.thebrpi.org/journals/jehd/Vol_4_No_4_December_2015/16.pdf; Nation 2007, The Four Strands (full text) — https://www.wgtn.ac.nz/lals/resources/paul-nations-resources/paul-nations-publications/publications/documents/2007-Four-strands.pdf

### Swain's output hypothesis rests on strong observational evidence (immersion learners' production deficit) and moderate experimental support for its noticing/hypothesis-testing functions.
Canadian French immersion students with thousands of hours of comprehensible input achieved near-native receptive skills but persistently non-native productive grammar — the canonical demonstration that input alone is insufficient. Output is theorized to work via noticing gaps, hypothesis testing, and metalinguistic reflection (Swain 1995, 2005); Izumi (2002) provided experimental support for the noticing function, and pushed-output studies show accuracy gains, but direct experimental tests of output-driven grammar learning have produced mixed results. Few dispute that output matters; how much of its effect is independent of the interaction/feedback it usually co-occurs with is less clear.
Sources: Nation 2007 (summarizing Swain 1985/1995/2005 and Izumi 2002) — https://www.wgtn.ac.nz/lals/resources/paul-nations-resources/paul-nations-publications/publications/documents/2007-Four-strands.pdf; Pushed output study (ERIC full text) — https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1127288.pdf; Review of Swain's approach, JALT — https://jalt-publications.org/tlt/articles/2198-output-and-beyond-dialogue-review-merrill-swains-current-approach-sla

### Conversational interaction with negotiation of meaning is among the most robust findings in instructed SLA, with large effects that grow on delayed posttests.
Keck et al. (2006, 14 studies) found large effects of task-based interaction on grammar and lexis, rising from immediate to short-delayed (d = 1.12) and long-delayed (d = 1.18) posttests. Mackey & Goo (2007, 28 studies) likewise found large effects, clearer on delayed posttests, and notably larger in foreign-language settings (d = 0.88) than second-language settings (d = 0.64) — encouraging for a learner practicing outside an immersion environment. Long's (1996) interaction hypothesis — that negotiation connects input, attention, and output — is now mainstream. Caveats: most studies target a small set of structures over short treatments, and Swan (2005) argues the interaction–acquisition link is thinner than proponents claim.
Sources: Keck et al. 2006 effect sizes summarized in a research review — https://files01.core.ac.uk/download/pdf/216985948.pdf; Mackey & Goo 2007, Semantic Scholar — https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Interaction-research-in-SLA-:-A-meta-analysis-and-Mackey-Goo/2b270feebe31f2f01e7c645cc00f8e94d504fb8b; Loewen & Sato, Interaction and instructed SLA (review) — https://www.researchgate.net/publication/325629431_Interaction_and_instructed_second_language_acquisition

### DeKeyser's skill acquisition theory is experimentally supported: explicitly learned grammar automatizes through practice along power-law curves — and automatization is skill-specific, so comprehension practice transfers poorly to production.
DeKeyser (1997) trained 61 learners on an artificial language over 8 weeks; reaction times and error rates followed the same power-function learning curves as other cognitive skills, and gains were highly specific to the practiced modality (comprehension practice sped comprehension, production practice sped production, with little cross-transfer). The declarative-to-procedural-to-automatic pathway (learn rule → use it in communicative practice → automatize through extensive meaning-focused use) is the standard model for adult, literate, explicit-instruction contexts. Contested edges: whether declarative knowledge is truly necessary (usage-based theorists say no) and whether lab findings with artificial languages scale to full L2s.
Sources: DeKeyser 1997, Beyond Explicit Rule Learning, SSLA (full PDF) — https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/B71581638BF89873F61F03EA1E747E5A/S0272263197002040a.pdf/beyond_explicit_rule_learning.pdf; DeKeyser & Suzuki 2025, Skill acquisition theory chapter — https://www.researchgate.net/publication/393631114; DeKeyser, Automatization, Skill Acquisition, and Practice (encyclopedia entry) — https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/9781405198431.wbeal0067

### Practice scheduling matters: distributed practice beats massed practice for retention, but for grammar proceduralization, relatively short gaps (about twice weekly) outperformed week-long gaps in the key L2 study.
Suzuki (2017) trained learners on morphology over four sessions and found shorter spacing (twice a week) produced better retention of procedural knowledge than once-a-week spacing — a caution against naively maximizing spacing for skill-type learning, and different from the very long optimal lags in vocabulary/fact memorization research. Suzuki & DeKeyser (2017) found aptitude-by-treatment interactions: language-analytic ability predicted outcomes under distributed practice, working memory under massed practice. This literature is young, lab-flavored, and should be treated as directional rather than settled.
Sources: Suzuki 2017, SSLA (author PDF) — https://yuichisuzuki.net/wp-content/uploads/2023/04/Suzuki-2018-SSLA.pdf; Suzuki & DeKeyser 2017, Applied Psycholinguistics — https://www.academia.edu/21992207; Suzuki, Nakata & DeKeyser 2019, Modern Language Journal special issue — https://www.academia.edu/40119025

### Oral corrective feedback works: medium-to-large, durable effects across two independent meta-analyses — Truscott's abandon-correction position is a minority view.
Li (2010; 33 studies) found an overall d = 0.64, maintained over time; explicit feedback beat implicit on immediate and short-delayed posttests, but implicit feedback (recasts) held up better on long-delayed posttests. Lyster & Saito (2010; 15 classroom studies, N = 827) found significant, durable effects, larger for prompts (elicitation, clarification requests, metalinguistic clues) than recasts, and most apparent on free constructed-response measures; longer treatments outperformed short-to-medium ones. Truscott (1999) argued oral grammar correction is ineffective and harmful and should be abandoned; Lyster et al.'s response and the subsequent meta-analytic record have largely carried the field, though Truscott maintains his position.
Sources: Li 2010, Language Learning — https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1467-9922.2010.00561.x; Lyster & Saito 2010, SSLA — https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/abs/oral-feedback-in-classroom-sla/4999EE1C8379B2BF026B148EAF373CA1; Lyster et al. response to Truscott — https://escholarship.mcgill.ca/concern/articles/gq67jv98t; Truscott interview, 25 years on — https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40862-021-00110-9

### The recasts-vs-prompts distinction has a clear mechanism: recasts are often not noticed as corrections, while prompts force retrieval and self-repair — aligning corrective feedback with the output/skill-acquisition account.
Recasts (interlocutor reformulates the error) are ambiguous — learners frequently hear them as conversational agreement, especially in meaning-focused talk and audio-only channels. Prompts withhold the correct form and push the learner to retrieve it, generating pushed output and successful uptake. Lyster & Saito's classroom data favor prompts; Li's data suggest explicitness helps short-term learning while implicit feedback may consolidate longer-term. Practical synthesis widely accepted: use prompts for forms the learner already partly knows (a rusty learner's dominant case), recasts or explicit correction for forms beyond current competence.
Sources: Lyster & Saito 2010 — https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/abs/oral-feedback-in-classroom-sla/4999EE1C8379B2BF026B148EAF373CA1; Li 2010 — https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1467-9922.2010.00561.x

### Comparative meta-analysis of comprehension-based vs production-based grammar instruction found both effective — input-only and output-only positions are both empirically untenable.
Shintani, Li & Ellis (2013; 35 comparisons in 30 studies) found substantial effects for both comprehension-based instruction (CBI, including VanPatten's Processing Instruction) and production-based instruction (PBI) on both receptive and productive knowledge. CBI tended to hold an edge on receptive measures; PBI was at least as good on productive measures. This directly undercuts strong claims from either the Krashen/VanPatten input camp or pure production camps, and supports mixed regimes.
Sources: Shintani, Li & Ellis 2013, Language Learning — https://www.researchgate.net/publication/236032380_Comprehension-Based_Versus_Production-Based_Grammar_Instruction_A_Meta-Analysis_of_Comparative_Studies; ERIC abstract — https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1135457

### TBLT is effective, but its headline effect size is disputed: d = 0.93 in the main meta-analysis fell to g = 0.61 under reanalysis, and theoretical critiques remain unanswered in part.
Bryfonski & McKay (2019; 52 studies of real TBLT program implementations) reported d = 0.93 across varied outcomes. Xuan, Cheung & Liu's technical comment identified loose inclusion criteria, oversimplified effect-size computation, and neglected moderators, recomputing a more modest g = 0.61 — still a medium-to-large effect by field benchmarks. Swan (2005, 'Legislation by Hypothesis') argued strong TBLT rests on unproven hypotheses (especially the noticing hypothesis and interaction-acquisition link), sidelines the teacher as a source of new language, and underserves grammar in limited-exposure foreign-language contexts; Long and Ellis have published defenses. Practical consensus: tasks with real outcomes plus focus-on-form beat both pure free conversation and pure grammar syllabi.
Sources: Bryfonski & McKay 2019, Language Teaching Research — https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/1362168817744389; Xuan, Cheung & Liu technical comment — https://doi.org/10.1177/13621688221131127; Swan 2005, Applied Linguistics — https://academic.oup.com/applij/article-abstract/26/3/376/181397; Ellis, In Defense of Tasks and TBLT — https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/annual-review-of-applied-linguistics/article/in-defense-of-tasks-and-tblt-nonissues-and-real-issues/ED5590DB727AE82E0A98AC05264B83E1

### Nation's Four Strands is the most cited evidence-grounded curriculum architecture: roughly 25% each of meaning-focused input, meaning-focused output, language-focused (deliberate) learning, and fluency development.
Nation (2007) specifies operating conditions for each strand: input must be 95–98% known vocabulary in large quantities; output must be meaning-driven communication about mostly familiar material; deliberate language-focused learning earns its 25% because it is roughly 4x more time-efficient than incidental learning (about 35 words/hour from paired-associate study vs a handful of words per hour of reading, per Waring & Takaki 2003); fluency work must involve zero new language, meaning focus, speed pressure, and volume. Nation concedes the equal-time split is 'an arbitrary decision' — it is a principled heuristic, not an empirically optimized allocation — and suggests proportions should shift with proficiency.
Sources: Nation 2007, The Four Strands (full text PDF) — https://www.wgtn.ac.nz/lals/resources/paul-nations-resources/paul-nations-publications/publications/documents/2007-Four-strands.pdf

### Fluency training via repeated speech under time pressure (4/3/2) reliably increases speech rate, and de Jong & Perfetti showed the gains reflect genuine proceduralization that transfers to new topics — but accuracy benefits are contested.
Nation (1989) and Arevart & Nation (1991) found 4/3/2 (same talk delivered in 4, then 3, then 2 minutes) increased fluency and reported accuracy/complexity gains. Boers (2014) and Thai & Boers (2016) challenged this: under increasing time pressure learners prioritize speed, and accuracy/complexity may stagnate or suffer. De Jong & Perfetti (2011) provided the strongest evidence: only learners who repeated the same topic across 4/3/2 cycles maintained fluency gains on posttests with new topics, indicating proceduralization of linguistic sequences rather than mere topic familiarity. Implication: repetition is the active ingredient; accuracy needs separate attention (e.g., feedback between retellings).
Sources: de Jong & Perfetti 2011, Language Learning — https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1467-9922.2010.00620.x; Preprint — https://research.vu.nl/ws/files/18460570/DeJong_Perfetti_LL_2011_preprint.pdf; Arevart & Nation 1991 — https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/003368829102200106; Effects of the 4/3/2 activity revisited (LTR) — http://kazuyasaito.net/LTR2021.pdf

### Pronunciation instruction produces large gains on targeted features in controlled tasks, but evidence that it improves global comprehensibility or accent in spontaneous speech is weak.
Lee, Jang & Plonsky (2015; 86 reports) found large overall effects (d ≈ 0.80–0.89), with larger effects for longer treatments, feedback-inclusive treatments, and controlled outcome measures. But most studies measured accuracy on trained features; only 7 of 75 showed significant gains in comprehensibility or intelligibility, and Saito & Plonsky's (2019) reanalysis found a general lack of significant effects on global speech dimensions. Perception-first training (e.g., high-variability minimal-pair discrimination) yields medium effects on both perception and production (Sakai & Moorman 2018). Bottom line for an accent goal: train specific, high-payoff features and measure comprehensibility, not global 'accent reduction'.
Sources: Lee, Jang & Plonsky 2015, Applied Linguistics — https://academic.oup.com/applij/article/36/3/345/2422438; Saito & Plonsky 2019, Language Learning — https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/lang.12345

### Bahrick's 50-year Spanish study: after exponential loss in years 1 through 3-6, remaining school-learned Spanish stabilizes in 'permastore' for decades, and the retained fraction depends chiefly on original training depth — very good news for a 4-on-AP learner 15 years out.
Bahrick (1984) tested 733 people up to 50 years after school Spanish. Memory declined exponentially for 3–6 years, then remained essentially flat for up to 30 years before a late-life decline. Retention was predictable from level of original training and grades, largely independent of rehearsal (most subjects rehearsed almost none). Recognition vocabulary and reading comprehension survived far better than recall. A learner 15 years post-AP is squarely on the flat permastore plateau: substantial knowledge is still there, mostly in recognition/receptive form. Caveat: cross-sectional design with self-reported training; but the pattern replicated in Bahrick & Phelps (1987) and later work.
Sources: Bahrick 1984, J Experimental Psychology: General (PubMed) — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/6242406/; Semantic Scholar record — https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Semantic-memory-content-in-permastore:-fifty-years-Bahrick/57f7bca4dbd92caba99c660b58f6d5013760ac35; Bahrick & Phelps 1987, 8-year retention (PDF) — https://gwern.net/doc/psychology/spaced-repetition/1987-bahrick.pdf

### The savings paradigm shows 'forgotten' language relearns much faster than new material — even items the learner can no longer recognize retain measurable residual activation.
De Bot & Stoessel (2000) and de Bot, Martens & Stoessel (2004) showed learners relearn once-known-but-forgotten vocabulary faster and retain it better than matched new words, even when pretests show no recognition. Hansen, Umeda & McKinney (2002), with 304 returned missionaries who had learned Japanese/Korean, found the strongest savings effect reported, though the advantage declines with time since learning and correlates with current vocabulary size (a Matthew effect: those who retained more relearn faster). Most savings research is lexical; savings for syntax is plausible (regression-hypothesis work suggests later-learned structures attrite first) but far less studied. Popular claims like 'relearning takes 50% of original time' are blog-level extrapolations, not established quantities.
Sources: Hansen, Umeda & McKinney 2002, Language Learning — https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/1467-9922.00200; de Bot, Martens & Stoessel 2004, International Journal of Bilingualism — https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/13670069040080031101; Review: Regaining the lost language through the savings paradigm — https://www.researchgate.net/publication/346322531

### Attrition hits productive skills and recall first and hardest; receptive knowledge and recognition are relatively spared — so a rusty learner's comprehension-production gap is the expected pattern, and production is where relearning effort should go.
The attrition literature (Bardovi-Harlig & Stringer 2010 review; Mehotcheva & Köpke; the Applied Psycholinguistics multilingual school-FL study) consistently finds early loss of oral fluency and lexical diversity, greater attrition of recall than recognition vocabulary, and little or no attrition of receptive abilities over comparable intervals; higher initial proficiency strongly protects against loss. The regression ('last in, first out') hypothesis has only limited empirical support. Pronunciation/motor patterns appear comparatively durable.
Sources: Bardovi-Harlig & Stringer, Variables in Second Language Attrition, SSLA — https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/abs/variables-in-second-language-attrition/541D9A111C251A98C4EDF33062438EC6; Attrition of school-learned foreign languages, Applied Psycholinguistics — https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/applied-psycholinguistics/article/attrition-of-schoollearned-foreign-languages-a-multilingual-perspective/EEF3FFDAEBCA439C1806DC3A370F34C8; Frontiers review bridging SLA and memory science — https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/human-neuroscience/articles/10.3389/fnhum.2019.00397/full

### Effect sizes across this literature must be read against field-specific benchmarks and pervasive study-quality problems: small samples, very short treatments, treatment-aligned tests, and scarce delayed posttests.
Plonsky & Oswald (2014), analyzing 346 primary studies and 91 meta-analyses (N > 604,000), derived L2-specific benchmarks: between-groups d ≈ 0.40 small, 0.70 medium, 1.00 large — meaning many 'large' SLA effects are ordinary by field standards. Typical primary studies run a few hours of treatment on one or two grammatical targets with researcher-made tests, and lab studies outperform classroom studies. No meta-analysis validates a complete long-duration curriculum; program-level evidence (TBLT evaluations) is the closest and is methodologically weakest. Rod Ellis's (2005) 'Principles of Instructed Language Learning' remains the most-cited consensus synthesis: build formulaic + rule-based competence, focus predominantly on meaning but also on form, develop mainly implicit knowledge without neglecting explicit knowledge, ensure extensive input and output opportunities, and assess free as well as controlled production.
Sources: Plonsky & Oswald 2014, Language Learning — https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/lang.12079; Ellis 2005, Principles of Instructed Language Learning (PDF) — http://www.drronmartinez.com/uploads/4/4/8/2/44820161/principles_of_instructed_language_learning_ellis_2005.pdf

## Design implications
- Structure the 500 hours roughly on Nation's four strands (~25% each): the AI conversation hour covers meaning-focused output/interaction; the plan must separately schedule large volumes of comprehensible input (podcasts, series, graded-to-authentic reading at 95-98% known vocabulary), deliberate language-focused study (grammar + spaced vocabulary), and dedicated fluency work — a conversation-only program would be unbalanced by the field's own consensus framework.
- Because automatization is skill-specific (DeKeyser 1997), speaking fluency requires speaking practice; the conversation-centered core is well supported, but listening-only or reading-only phases will not transfer to speaking speed.
- Phase 1 (~first 50-100 hours) should be reactivation, not beginner instruction: savings and permastore research predicts rapid recovery of AP-era material, with recognition/comprehension returning before recall/production; run the AI sessions as forced-production reactivation (retrieval of old vocabulary and structures in speech) rather than re-teaching, and expect the comprehension-production gap to be temporary.
- Engineer the AI's corrective feedback explicitly in the session prompt: default to prompts (clarification requests, elicitation, metalinguistic clues like 'was that the right past tense?') for forms the learner partly knows — the dominant case for a rusty learner — reserving recasts/explicit correction for genuinely new forms; audio-only recasts are especially likely to go unnoticed, so require the AI to flag corrections saliently or hold an explicit end-of-session error review.
- Build sessions around tasks with outcomes (information gap, narrative retelling, role-play with a goal, spot-the-difference), not open-ended chat — the interaction/TBLT literature's effects come from negotiation of meaning under communicative pressure; also instruct the AI to sometimes feign non-understanding, since a maximally accommodating interlocutor removes the communication breakdowns that drive negotiation.
- Include a 4/3/2-style block regularly: same monologue delivered 2-3 times with shrinking time limits, repeating the SAME topic across a session (de Jong & Perfetti's transfer condition), with feedback on accuracy between retellings to offset the technique's accuracy blind spot.
- Externalize curriculum memory since each ChatGPT session starts fresh: maintain a living state document (current level notes, persistent-error log, target structures, vocabulary review queue, next task type) pasted or read at session start, and harvest each session's errors/new items into it afterward — this substitutes for the cumulative teacher knowledge that the corrective-feedback and TBLT literatures implicitly assume.
- Run explicit grammar study (it has the largest measured effects) but evaluate it in free production: track error rates per 100 words in spontaneous speech samples over time, not performance on drills, to avoid the outcome-measure artifact that inflates explicit-instruction results.
- Space practice deliberately but not maximally: for proceduralizing grammar and speech routines, sessions at least 2-3x/week (Suzuki's short-lag advantage) beat weekly sessions; for vocabulary, use a standard expanding-interval SRS fed from session transcripts — the two systems have different optimal schedules.
- For accent: use perception-first training (minimal-pair discrimination of Spanish problem sounds for English speakers) plus targeted feature-by-feature production feedback; set the goal as comprehensibility on specific features (vowel purity, /r/-/rr/, intonation), not global 'accent elimination', which the meta-analytic evidence does not support; note that AI judgment of subtle accent features via voice models is itself unvalidated and should be triangulated with recordings compared against native models.
- Assessment architecture: schedule periodic timed free-speech samples (recorded), scored on complexity-accuracy-fluency metrics (syllables/minute, pauses, errors/100 words, clause complexity) plus delayed retention checks — the literature's clearest lesson is that immediate performance overstates learning, so every 'learned' item needs a delayed test.
- Calibrate expectations with field benchmarks: meta-analytic effects (d ≈ 0.6-1.1) come from hours-long interventions on narrow targets; 500 well-structured hours is a genuinely large dose by research standards, but no study validates any specific full curriculum — treat the plan as evidence-informed engineering, not an evidence-prescribed protocol, and iterate on the learner's own measured data.

## Open questions
- Does interaction with an AI voice interlocutor confer the same acquisition benefits as human negotiation of meaning? The interaction meta-analyses are all human-human; early neurocognitive work (Li & Jeong 2020) suggests social feedback matters, and an always-comprehending AI may under-supply genuine communication breakdowns.
- Does the explicit-instruction advantage survive when outcomes are spontaneous oral production? Kang et al. (2019) suggest the gap narrows with free-response measures; the field has not resolved whether explicit knowledge converts to implicit knowledge (the interface debate) or merely coexists with it.
- Are recasts noticed at all in audio-only conversation with an AI, where the visual/prosodic cues that signal 'this is a correction' are degraded? No direct research exists.
- How large is the savings effect for grammar/syntax (as opposed to vocabulary), and how fast does productive syntax recover after 15 years? Nearly all savings research is lexical; the regression hypothesis has limited empirical support.
- What is the optimal spacing schedule across a 500-hour, multi-month program? Lab findings (short lags better for proceduralization, long lags better for declarative retention) have not been tested at curriculum scale.
- Do 4/3/2 accuracy/complexity gains replicate? Nation (1989)/Arevart & Nation (1991) found accuracy gains; Boers (2014) and Thai & Boers (2016) found fluency-only gains or trade-offs.
- How much of TBLT's measured effect is task design versus sheer time-on-task speaking? The Bryfonski & McKay vs Xuan et al. dispute leaves the true program-level effect uncertain (g ≈ 0.6-0.9).
- Can consumer voice models reliably evaluate grammar and accent in real time? Their error-detection precision/recall on L2 Spanish speech is unmeasured in the academic literature, so an external validation loop (recordings, periodic human or test-based benchmarks like ACTFL OPI/SIELE) may be needed.
