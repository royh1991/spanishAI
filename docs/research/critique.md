# Critique

## Gaps
- Motivation, adherence, and dropout for self-directed learners — the single largest failure risk of a 500-hour solo plan and no agent researched it. There is a real literature here: self-regulated language learning, goal-setting and implementation-intention research, habit formation, and the dismal completion rates of self-study formats (MOOC completion ~5-10%; app-based learner persistence similarly low). The MTC agent correctly notes the motivation engine is 'least transferable' and offers substitutes, but those substitutes (weekly reports to a human, tied-to-trips purpose) are asserted, not researched. Nobody addressed what predicts 18-month adherence in adults with jobs, or evidence on accountability mechanisms (paid tutors as commitment devices, public commitment, streak vs. outcome tracking without gamification).
- Listening-skill training as its own discipline. The program covers conversation, HVPT perception drills, and 'listen to podcasts,' but no agent covered the listening pedagogy literature: decoding connected speech and reductions (a distinct bottleneck for fast colloquial Mexican Spanish), rate-manipulated/slowed listening evidence, the large captioned-video literature (e.g., Montero Perez et al. meta-analyses on captions and L2 listening/vocabulary), extensive listening research, or transcription/dictation training. Listening is also nearly absent from the assessment battery, which is speaking-obsessed — yet FSI's own post-2022 test weights listening ~50%, a fact the gov agent reports without anyone drawing the training implication.
- Reading and writing as learning tools, not just assessment channels. Extensive reading has its own meta-analytic literature (graded readers, Jeon & Day 2016) and is the cheapest source of the 95-98%-coverage input Nation's framework demands; the written corrective feedback literature (Bitchener, Ferris, the separate written-CF Truscott debate) is entirely missing even though written CF is where an LLM tutor is demonstrably strongest (text, no ASR distortion). Writing appears only as 'the cheapest reliably-measured skill' — no one asked whether daily writing with correction should be a core learning activity for a false-intermediate fixing fossilized grammar.
- Spanish-specific morphosyntax acquisition research. All grammar evidence cited is language-general; nothing on acquisition sequences/processability for the structures that will dominate this learner's 500 hours: subjunctive development, preterite/imperfect and the aspect hypothesis literature, clitic acquisition, ser/estar development. This matters for sequencing (what is learnable at B1+ vs. what resists instruction) and for setting realistic error-log targets.
- Fossilization/defossilization evidence. Multiple agents invoke Higgs & Clifford's warning that communicative practice automatizes errors in false intermediates — the learner's exact profile — but no agent researched whether and how fossilized errors can actually be remediated, or the effectiveness of the proposed remedy (error logs + recurring focus-on-form). The plan's central risk is asserted; its mitigation is folklore.
- Transfer from AI conversation to human conversation. Several agents note ASR silently normalizes learner errors and that the AI is an over-accommodating interlocutor, but no one researched whether speaking gains with AI partners transfer to human interlocutors under real conditions (background noise, impatience, dialect variation, multiparty). The chatbot meta-analyses cited are short EFL classroom studies with researcher-administered tests, not transfer studies. Given the whole architecture rests on this substitution, the absence of transfer evidence should be stated as a first-order unknown, and periodic human-conversation benchmarks framed as hypothesis tests, not garnish.
- Burnout, plateaus, and periodization over 12-18 months. The gov agent recommends 10-15+ h/week with 'surge periods' based on full-time, salaried, selected learners; nothing addresses sustainability for a working adult, the well-known intermediate/B2 plateau, deliberate rest, or what to do when measured progress stalls for months (which the assessment schedule will make visible). Attrition of the learner, not the language, is unmodeled.
- Measurement sensitivity for n=1 longitudinal use. The assessment battery is built from instruments validated for discriminating groups (EIT's g=1.34 mostly separates advanced from beginner groups), not for detecting one learner's month-over-month change. No agent addressed standard error of measurement, minimum detectable change, or practice effects quantitatively — so the plan may generate noisy scores that falsely signal plateau or progress. Related: no inter-form equivalence data for home-made monologue prompts or self-scored CAF metrics.
- Cost and contingency planning. The caps analysis implies the planned 1 h/day of GPT-Live-1 requires at minimum Plus with zero slack, realistically Pro ($100-200/mo per current reporting) — plus LTI OPIc fees, tutor hours, and Azure usage. No agent totaled this (~$2,000-4,500 over 18 months) or researched fallback voice-AI options (Gemini Live, other real-time voice products) if OpenAI changes limits, which every agent agrees happens without notice. Also unaddressed: privacy implications of recording and uploading hundreds of hours of voice data.
- Dialect targeting. The learner's use case is Mexico, but no agent researched Mexican-Spanish-specific input sources, whether GPT-Live-1 can sustain a consistent Mexican variety, or how dialect mixing in materials (Castilian graded readers, Colombian podcast hosts) affects a learner at this level. Only passing mentions (Langua dialect voices, Azure es-MX).

## Dubious claims
- CLAIM: ai-architecture: sycophancy findings ('+49.3% vs +4.8% improvement; 71% could not detect it') applied to language tutoring
  WHY: The study is real — I verified 'Invisible Saboteurs' (CHI 2026, arXiv 2510.03667) exists and matches the description — but it is a within-subjects n=24 experiment on debugging ML models with artificially engineered high/low-sycophancy bots. The percentages are task-specific point estimates from 24 people; '71%' is 17 participants. Directionally useful for tutor-prompt design, but citing these numbers as quantified effect sizes for L2 correction behavior is overreach.
- CLAIM: ai-architecture: 'No credible AI phoneme-scoring product for Spanish surfaced in this research'
  WHY: Contradicted by the assessment agent, which is correct: Azure Pronunciation Assessment supports Spanish (es-ES, es-MX) with phoneme-level scoring, and Speechace-class engines also cover Spanish. The ai-architecture agent searched consumer apps (ELSA, BoldVoice, Gliglish) and generalized from their English-only status. This is an incomplete-research artifact, and its design implication (route all Spanish accent work to human coaches) is stronger than warranted.
- CLAIM: assessment: elicited imitation as the recurring monthly 'objective dipstick' (Yan et al. 2016 g=1.34; r=.66 with proficiency)
  WHY: The meta-analytic numbers are accurately quoted (I verified Yan et al. 2016), but g=1.34 reflects EI discriminating between groups at distant proficiency bands (mostly advanced vs. beginner/intermediate). Sensitivity to one learner's month-over-month change is unestablished, Solon et al. found ceiling compression at advanced levels (exactly where this learner is headed), and repeated administration invites practice effects that parallel forms only partially fix. EI is a reasonable anchor 2-3 times across the program; as a monthly progress meter it will likely be noise-dominated.
- CLAIM: vocab-srs: 'FSRS beats SM-2 for >99% of users' and saves '20-30% of reviews'
  WHY: The benchmark exists but is run by FSRS's own developers on logs from self-selected Anki users, measures log-loss of recall prediction (not learning outcomes), and bolts a probability interpretation onto SM-2 that it was never designed for — a caveat the agent notes and then discards in the design implications. No experimental study shows FSRS produces better retention per hour. The right conclusion (any modern SRS is fine; don't over-optimize) is actually stated elsewhere in the same stream, making the '>99%' framing rhetorical.
- CLAIM: mtc-missionary: Radford (2014) shows 'simply teaching the speaking-performance rubric increased proficiency'
  WHY: A single unreplicated BYU dissertation from inside the institution being studied, with a small 2x2 design and partly self-reported mechanisms. Rubric transparency is plausibly useful and consistent with formative-assessment literature, but a lone dissertation cannot support the causal phrasing 'rubric knowledge alone improved proficiency' that survives into the design implications.
- CLAIM: mtc-missionary: Dewey & Clifford (2012) '~80% of returned missionaries at Advanced-Mid/Advanced-High, one full level above graduating language majors'
  WHY: All measurement is post-hoc on self-selected recently-returned missionaries tested by community-affiliated researchers; the comparison with language majors confounds selection (missionaries had 4,000-6,000 hours of immersive use; majors did not), and the agent itself concedes no controlled comparisons exist. The number may be roughly right, but it is repeatedly used across streams as a calibration anchor ('B2 took 4,000-6,000 hours') with more precision than the evidence supports.
- CLAIM: gov-intensive: Serrano & Muñoz concentration findings justify 'surge periods' and a 10-15+ h/week minimum viable intensity
  WHY: Serrano & Muñoz compared intact EFL program formats (intensive vs. regular courses) without random assignment; selection into intensive courses is confounded with motivation. The broader distributed-practice literature — cited by the same agent — is mixed-to-opposed. The honest version ('high weekly density beats a trickle') is defensible; the specific numeric threshold and surge-period prescription are extrapolations dressed as findings.
- CLAIM: sla-evidence design implication: 'Run explicit grammar study (it has the largest measured effects)'
  WHY: The stream's own findings section carefully documents that the explicit-instruction advantage is substantially a measurement artifact (constrained tests rewarding explicit knowledge; Kang, Sok & Han 2019 — which I verified — found only a minor explicit-implicit difference once outcome measures were modeled). The design implication then quietly reverts to the inflated headline. The right claim is 'explicit grammar study is worthwhile and efficient, but its meta-analytic effect sizes overstate its impact on spontaneous speech.'
- CLAIM: ai-architecture: Kamelabad et al. null result licenses 'recast in the moment, full review at end — sacrifices nothing measurable'
  WHY: A non-significant difference in one semester-long study with n=65-66 is weak evidence of equivalence (no power analysis reported, grammar-only outcomes, text-capable chatbots rather than full-duplex voice). Treating an underpowered null as an affirmative license is a classic misreading. It also sits awkwardly with the sla stream's evidence that audio-only recasts specifically tend to go unnoticed — the modality where this plan lives.
- CLAIM: Cross-stream: 'plausibly re-testable at ILR 1+/2 after reactivation' and 'first 50-100 hours of aggressive production-forced reactivation' presented as evidence-based
  WHY: The savings/permastore literature (Bahrick; de Bot; Hansen) is almost entirely about lexical recognition and relearning speed of vocabulary. That relearning is fast is well supported; that production-forcing is the optimal reactivation mode, that syntax shows comparable savings, and any specific level estimate or phase length are extrapolations. The agents' own caveats say this ('savings for syntax is far less studied'), but the design implications state phase-1 structure with unearned confidence.
- CLAIM: gov-intensive: ILR S-3 described as 'roughly upper-B2/C1' while also 'roughly ACTFL Superior'
  WHY: Internally slippery and inconsistent with the assessment stream's (correct) report that no verified three-way ILR/ACTFL/CEFR mapping exists and that ACTFL's crosswalk puts Advanced High ↔ C1, with Superior above that. Calling ILR 3 'upper-B2' makes FSI's target sound closer to this learner's reach than the conventional ILR3≈Superior≈C1+ reading, subtly inflating the feasibility of 'overshoot borderline 3' advice.
- CLAIM: ai-architecture: GPT-Live-1 specifics (GPT-5.5 delegation, GPQA 84.2% vs 45.3%, exact tier caps)
  WHY: I verified the load-bearing parts: launch July 8, 2026 and the Plus caps (1 h Instant + 1 h Medium/High + 2 h mini per rolling 24 h; Pro 12/12/24; top tier unlimited) match current help-center reporting — so this claim largely survives scrutiny. Remaining soft spots: the GPQA figures and background-delegation details trace to launch coverage, not independent testing; the agent admits it never fetched the help-center page directly; and the practical implication deserves emphasis — on Plus, the top model covers the planned hour with zero slack only by spending both intelligence buckets, and the numbers are explicitly subject to change.

## Contradictions
- Spanish pronunciation scoring: the assessment stream says dedicated ASR scoring for Spanish exists and should be the accent-feedback channel (Azure Pronunciation Assessment es-ES/es-MX, Speechace-class engines); the ai-architecture stream flatly states no credible AI phoneme-scoring product for Spanish exists and routes accent work to Speechling human coaches. The assessment stream is right; a plan-writer following ai-architecture would omit a usable tool.
- 500-hour target level: assessment sets the realistic target at ACTFL Advanced Mid (~B2.2) and calls it 'ambitious but defensible'; gov-intensive tells the learner to 'design the 500 hours to overshoot borderline ILR 3' (Superior-equivalent) and calls 500 hours 'tight' even for solid S-3; mtc-missionary reports B2 took missionaries 4,000-6,000 hours of immersive use and only 6% reached Superior. These imply mutually inconsistent endpoints — a plan cannot simultaneously treat B2.2 as the realistic ceiling, Superior as the retention-mandated floor, and B2 as a multi-thousand-hour outcome.
- Conversation-hour share of the budget: the user premise builds the plan 'around ~1-hour conversation sessions,' sla-evidence allocates conversation ~25% as the output strand, gov-intensive prescribes 150-200 of 500 hours (30-40%) of live conversation inside a 2.5-3h daily block, and vocab-srs is ambiguous about whether ~120-180 SRS hours sit inside or alongside the 500. No two streams use the same accounting frame for what the '500 hours' contains.
- Practice distribution: gov-intensive's headline is concentration (intensity crucial, concentrated hours beat spread, plan surges); vocab-srs's headline is very long spacing (8-week gaps 'most durable schedule ever tested', multi-week theme recycling); sla-evidence prescribes short 2-3x/week lags for grammar proceduralization. These are reconcilable (macro-intensity vs. within-item spacing vs. skill-type differences) but no stream performs the reconciliation, and each states its principle in general terms a plan-writer could apply globally.
- Default corrective-feedback move in voice sessions: sla-evidence argues prompts (elicitation, clarification) should be the default because audio-only recasts are especially likely to go unnoticed, and Lyster & Saito's data favor prompts; ai-architecture's recommended protocol defaults to 'recast then continue' with a consolidated end review, leaning on one null-result chatbot study. The two streams hand the session designer opposite defaults for the same moment.
- One-on-one sufficiency: gov-intensive reports FSI's explicit position that pure tutorial one-on-one training is NOT best for most learners and that multiparty conversation is the hardest, least-trained skill; mtc-missionary attributes the strongest outcome variance to live native companions. Both then bolt patches onto an architecture whose core (solo learner + one AI interlocutor) their own primary sources warn against — the tension is acknowledged in fragments but never confronted as a structural risk.
- Pre-teaching vocabulary: vocab-srs's session loop prescribes pre-loading 5-10 target words and instructing the AI to push their use, while the same stream (and the TBLT position in sla-evidence) warns that front-loading vocabulary converts tasks into presentation lessons and that breakdown-driven 'just-in-time' vocabulary is at least as memorable; mtc-missionary's SYL model is purely reactive. The prescriptions pull in opposite directions and the conflict is never adjudicated.

## Overall
This is unusually strong multi-agent research: primary sources dominate, effect sizes are mostly quoted accurately (I verified Kang/Sok/Han 2019's 'minor explicit-implicit difference', Yan et al. 2016's g=1.34, the CHI 2026 sycophancy study's existence and design, and the GPT-Live-1 launch date and tier caps against current reporting), and the agents flag many of their own weaknesses — the meta-analytic short-treatment problem, BYU-affiliated evidence, unstable product limits. The recurring failure mode is not fabrication but caveat decay: findings sections state limitations honestly, then design implications restate the inflated headline (explicit instruction's 'largest effects', production-forced reactivation, surge periods, FSRS superiority, recast-by-default). The two most consequential unresolved problems are (1) the three-way contradiction on what 500 hours can buy (B2.2 vs. 'overshoot ILR 3' vs. 'B2 costs 4,000-6,000 hours') — the plan-writer must pick one target and defend it, and the honest pick is Advanced-Low-to-Mid speaking with Superior functions as probes; and (2) the complete absence of motivation/adherence research, which for an 18-month solo program is more likely to determine the outcome than any pedagogical choice the six agents optimized. Secondary gaps that materially change the plan: listening-decoding training, extensive reading and written corrective feedback as core (not assessment) activities, n=1 measurement sensitivity, cost/contingency for the AI dependency, and defossilization evidence for the plan's central false-intermediate risk.
