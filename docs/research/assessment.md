# assessment

## Summary
Spanish proficiency for this learner is best framed on three interlocking scales: ACTFL (the US academic scale behind the Oral Proficiency Interview), ILR (the US government 0-5 scale ACTFL was derived from), and CEFR (A1-C2). ACTFL's own official crosswalk — established through test-by-test standard-setting studies, not construct mapping — puts spoken Advanced Low at CEFR B2.1, Intermediate High at B1.2, and Intermediate Mid at B1.1, and ACTFL is explicit that these correspondences are one-directional and that no verified three-way ACTFL/ILR/CEFR table exists. The OPI itself is a highly structured 15-30 minute interview with four mandatory phases (warm-up, level checks, probes, wind-down): level checks establish the "floor" (the highest level sustained across ALL criteria — functions, context/content, accuracy, text type), probes push one major level higher until "linguistic breakdown" reveals the ceiling, and a role-play adds a transactional task. This protocol is well-documented and largely replicable in spirit by an AI interviewer, even though only an LTI-administered, double-rated interview yields an official rating. For this learner, ACTFL's published guidance maps an AP Spanish Language score of 4 to roughly Intermediate High (with 3 ≈ Intermediate Mid and Advanced-range performance most likely behind a 5) — i.e., roughly CEFR B1+ in speaking at age ~18 — though ACTFL itself labels this mapping "anecdotal evidence and examination of the scoring rubric," not a validated equating study.

Fifteen years of disuse is less catastrophic than intuition suggests. The attrition literature (Bahrick's 50-year "permastore" study of school-learned Spanish; the Schmid & Mehotcheva review) consistently finds that loss is front-loaded (most attrition occurs in the first 3-6 years, then plateaus for decades), that productive skills and lexical access decline while receptive knowledge stays largely intact, that attained proficiency at the end of study is the strongest predictor of retention, and that "savings" make relearning much faster than initial learning. A false-intermediate who tested at IH/B1+ and still "gets by" in Mexico plausibly sits around Intermediate Low-Mid in unrehearsed speaking today, with reading/listening closer to their old level, and should expect rapid early re-gains. Against FSI benchmarks (Spanish is Category I: 600-750 intensive class hours from zero to ILR 3/Superior-ish; ETS/FSI-derived tables put learners at Intermediate High-Advanced Low around 480 cumulative hours), a 500-hour plan is realistically aimed at consolidating Advanced (B2-C1 threshold) speaking — an ambitious but defensible target given savings effects.

For self-run assessment, the strongest evidence supports a battery rather than any single instrument: elicited imitation tests are the best-validated quick proxy for global oral proficiency (meta-analyses: Hedges' g = 1.34 for discriminating levels; average r = .66 with other proficiency measures), and a validated 30-item Spanish EIT (Ortega et al. 2002; revisited by Solon et al. 2019, with parallel forms now studied) exists and is scriptable; NCSSFL-ACTFL Can-Do statements work as goal-setting and self-assessment scaffolds but discriminate poorly among higher sublevels (Rasch analysis found 15/50 misfitting items), and self-assessment generally correlates only ~.45-.47 with tested proficiency, improving when tied to concrete tasks and rubrics. LLMs are credible-but-biased raters of transcribed language: GPT-4-class models rate CEFR writing near specialist AES systems when given calibration examples, and a 2025 Cambridge study got r ≈ 0.76 with human holistic scores applying CEFR descriptors zero-shot to speech transcripts (vs 0.82 for a fine-tuned speech model) — but agreement is study-dependent (~.30-.80), leniency/severity biases are real and inconsistent across studies, and transcripts erase exactly the phonological information accent assessment needs.

Accent is where the AI-conversation-partner architecture has a genuine hole. The pronunciation-training literature is moderately encouraging for adults: pronunciation instruction shows a large mean effect in Lee, Jang & Plonsky's meta-analysis (d = 0.80, mostly on controlled tasks), explicit phonetics instruction improves Spanish learners' production and perception of the classic English-speaker problem areas (unaspirated /p t k/, the approximants [β ð ɣ], tap vs trill /ɾ r/, and resisting English vowel reduction in Spanish's five-vowel system), HVPT reliably improves perception (g ≈ 0.67-0.92) with smaller, less durable transfer to production, and shadowing helps prosody and comprehensibility. But the realistic goal for an adult is comprehensible, low-friction speech, not a native accent — accentedness is the most change-resistant dimension. Critically, current LLM voice models cannot do fine-grained phonetic judgment: on benchmark tests GPT-4o performed at chance (≤33%) on pronunciation-similarity judgments, and its pronunciation scores correlate far worse with human raters (PCC ~0.28-0.58 by granularity) than dedicated ASR systems (~0.69-0.81), with high failure rates. So GPT-Live-1 should be treated as a conversation partner and content/grammar evaluator, while pronunciation feedback should run through a separate loop: ASR-based tools that support Spanish phoneme-level scoring (e.g., Azure Pronunciation Assessment with es-MX models, apps built on it or on Speechace), minimal-pair/HVPT-style perception drills, shadowing with self-recording against native audio, and an occasional human check (tutor or official OPIc) to anchor the whole system.

## Findings
### The ACTFL OPI is a four-phase structured interview (warm-up, level checks, probes, wind-down) that rates speakers by establishing a 'floor' (highest level sustained across all criteria) and a 'ceiling' (where linguistic breakdown occurs under probing).
Per ACTFL's official OPI Familiarization Guide (2020): level checks elicit the floor — sustained performance across ALL four assessment criteria (global functions, context/content, accuracy/comprehensibility, text type) at a level; probes raise tasks to the next major level until breakdown reveals the ceiling; a role-play (transactional/social situation) serves as an extra level check or probe; the wind-down returns the speaker to a comfortable level. One of ten ratings (Novice Low through Superior) is then assigned against the Proficiency Guidelines 2012 - Speaking. Official ratings require an LTI-employed certified tester plus blind double rating with exact agreement. Major-level criteria: Intermediate = create with language, ask/answer simple questions, discrete sentences, understood by sympathetic listeners; Advanced = narrate/describe in all major time frames, handle a complication, paragraph-length discourse, understood by natives unaccustomed to learners; Superior = support opinion, hypothesize, abstract topics, extended discourse, no pattern of basic errors.
Sources: ACTFL OPI Familiarization Guide 2020 (read in full): https://www.actfl.org/uploads/files/general/OPI-Familiarization-Guide-2020-1.pdf; ACTFL OPI ACE report: https://www.actfl.org/uploads/files/general/Documents/assessments/acereports/OPI_Report_Part_A_General_Information_2023.pdf; ACTFL OPI page: https://www.actfl.org/assessments/postsecondary-assessments/opi

### ACTFL's official ACTFL-CEFR crosswalk (for speaking) maps Advanced Low ↔ B2.1, Intermediate High ↔ B1.2, Intermediate Mid ↔ B1.1, Intermediate Low ↔ A2, Advanced Mid ↔ B2.2, Advanced High ↔ C1 — and is explicitly one-directional and test-based, not a construct equivalence.
The ACTFL document 'Assigning CEFR Ratings to ACTFL Assessments' (read in full) reports that the OPI/OPIc were linked to CEFR via the Council of Europe's standard-setting approach (Bärenfänger & Tschirner 2012); receptive-skill correspondences differ (e.g., reading/listening Advanced Mid ↔ B2, Intermediate High ↔ B1.1). ACTFL stresses that frameworks 'can only be aligned on a test by test basis' and that no CEFR-based test has been linked to the ACTFL framework, so CEFR-to-ACTFL claims are unsupported. Independent commentary confirms there are no verified three-way ACTFL/ILR/CEFR equivalencies. The ACTFL scale itself was developed from the government ILR 0-5 scale; conventional (but informal) speaking correspondences are Advanced ≈ ILR 2, Superior ≈ ILR 3.
Sources: ACTFL, Assigning CEFR Ratings to ACTFL Assessments (PDF, read in full): https://www.actfl.org/uploads/files/general/Assigning_CEFR_Ratings_To_ACTFL_Assessments.pdf; ACTFL page: https://www.actfl.org/assessments/assigning-cefr-ratings-to-actfl-assessments; ILR skill level descriptions: https://www.govtilr.org/Skills/ILRscale1.htm; LTI ACTFL scale explainer: https://www.languagetesting.com/actfl-proficiency-scale

### ACTFL's published guidance maps an AP Spanish Language score of 4 to roughly Intermediate High (score 3 ≈ Intermediate Mid; Advanced-level evidence most likely yields a 5) — i.e., about CEFR B1+/B1.2 in speaking — but ACTFL itself calls this mapping 'anecdotal evidence and examination of the scoring rubric,' not a validated equating.
An ACTFL Seal-of-Biliteracy advisory document (read in full) states: 'students who are showing evidence of Intermediate Mid proficiency are likely to score a 3... students showing evidence of Intermediate High proficiency are likely to score a 4 or 5; students showing evidence of Advanced level proficiency are the most likely to score a 5.' The College Board's course description says AP Spanish targets the Intermediate-to-Pre-Advanced range (roughly 3-4 college semesters). Combining with the ACTFL-CEFR speaking crosswalk, a 4 ≈ Intermediate High ≈ B1.2 productive skills at the time of testing. No formal College Board-ACTFL concordance study for AP world-language scores was found; this is a genuine evidence gap.
Sources: ACTFL Seal of Biliteracy comparison-of-scales talking points (PDF, read in full): https://www.actfl.org/uploads/files/general/SealofBiliteracy-ComparisonofScales-TalkingPointsv3.pdf; AP Spanish Language and Culture Course and Exam Description: https://apcentral.collegeboard.org/media/pdf/ap-spanish-language-and-culture-course-and-exam-description.pdf; College Board Validated Achievement Level Descriptions: https://apcentral.collegeboard.org/media/pdf/ap-world-languages-achievement-level-descriptions-dec-2013.pdf

### Language attrition after long disuse is front-loaded and partial: most loss occurs in the first 3-6 years then plateaus for decades, productive skills and lexical access suffer most, receptive knowledge is largely retained, and higher attained proficiency strongly predicts retention.
Bahrick (1984) tested 733 people up to 50 years after school Spanish: memory curves decline exponentially for 3-6 years, then remain stable for up to 30 years ('permastore'); retention was predicted by level of original training and grades. The Schmid & Mehotcheva review (read in full) summarizes: productive skills are more vulnerable than receptive (Weltens 1988; Hakuta & D'Andrea 1992); attrition sets in rapidly then levels off; time alone does not predict loss; once a language is 'sufficiently established/entrenched, it becomes impervious to deterioration' (interpreted as a critical threshold — Neisser 1984); adult attriters rarely show drastic proficiency drops (unlike child learners); lexical/declarative knowledge is affected before proceduralized grammar and phonology. The savings paradigm shows seemingly lost material is relearned much faster than new material. Caveat: instructed-FL attrition studies are few, and self-reported loss typically exceeds objectively measured loss.
Sources: Bahrick 1984 (PubMed): https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/6242406/; Bahrick 1987 8-year retention: https://gwern.net/doc/psychology/spaced-repetition/1987-bahrick.pdf; Schmid & Mehotcheva, Foreign language attrition (PDF, read pp.1-15): https://languageattrition.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/05/schmidmehotcheva2012.pdf

### Given AP-4 (~Intermediate High/B1+) at age ~18 and 15 years of near-disuse with occasional travel use, the learner most plausibly sits around ACTFL Intermediate Low-Mid in spontaneous speaking today, with listening/reading better preserved — and should expect unusually fast early re-gains from savings/relearning effects.
This is an inference from converging evidence rather than a directly measured finding: attrition research predicts loss concentrated in productive fluency and lexical retrieval with receptive skills and proceduralized grammar largely intact; the learner's original level (IH) sits near the 'critical threshold' that Bahrick and Neisser associate with long-term retention; and occasional rehearsal (travel in Mexico) further reduces expected loss. The savings literature (de Bot, Martens & Stoessel 2004; Hansen 2011) implies vocabulary and structures will reactivate faster than a true intermediate learner could acquire them. An actual placement measurement (EIT + simulated OPI + can-do inventory) should replace this estimate in week 1 of the program.
Sources: Schmid & Mehotcheva review: https://languageattrition.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/05/schmidmehotcheva2012.pdf; Savings in relearning L2 vocabulary: https://www.academia.edu/26655506/Savings_in_the_Relearning_of_Second_Language_Vocabulary_The_Effects_of_Time_and_Proficiency; Bahrick 1984: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/6242406/

### FSI/ETS-derived hour benchmarks make ~500 hours a credible budget for moving a recovering B1+ learner into the Advanced (B2-C1 threshold) range in Spanish, a Category I language.
FSI estimates 600-750 intensive classroom hours take a zero-beginner to ILR 3 (Professional Working Proficiency) in Category I languages like Spanish. The ETS/FSI-derived table republished by Language Testing International shows average-aptitude learners reaching Intermediate High-Advanced Low around 480 cumulative hours and Advanced Mid-Superior around 720 hours. These figures assume intensive small-group instruction plus homework, so self-directed AI conversation hours are not strictly equivalent; but the learner is not starting from zero, and savings effects work in their favor. Realistic 500-hour target: solid Advanced Mid speaking (≈B2.2 by ACTFL's crosswalk), with Superior/C1 functions emerging but probably not sustained.
Sources: US State Dept foreign language training: https://www.state.gov/national-foreign-affairs-training-center/foreign-language-training; LTI 'How long does it take': https://www.languagetesting.com/how-long-does-it-take

### NCSSFL-ACTFL Can-Do statements are useful self-assessment and goal-setting scaffolds, but their measurement validity is thin, they discriminate poorly among higher sublevels, and self-assessment generally correlates only moderately (r ≈ .45-.47) with tested proficiency.
Tigchelaar et al. (2017) had 382 university Spanish learners self-assess on 50 speaking Can-Do statements: Rasch analysis found 15 misfitting items, and while mean item difficulties ascended correctly through lower sublevels, the instrument 'did not discriminate well between statements pegged at higher proficiency sublevels.' Li & Zhang's (2021) meta-analysis of 67 studies (68,500+ participants) found an overall self-assessment/performance correlation of .466, rising toward ~.49 when self-assessment used concrete real-life task descriptors, rubrics/criterion-referencing, and training. Implication: Can-Dos work best as a curriculum checklist and progress log, not as the primary proficiency measure.
Sources: Tigchelaar et al. 2017, Foreign Language Annals: https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/flan.12286 (PDF: https://languagetesting.com/pub/media/wysiwyg/research/articles/Tigchelaar_et_al-2017-Foreign_Language_Annals.pdf); Li & Zhang 2021, Language Testing: https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/0265532220932481; ACTFL Can-Do statements: https://www.actfl.org/educator-resources/ncssfl-actfl-can-do-statements

### Elicited imitation (EI) is the best-validated quick objective proxy for global oral proficiency — meta-analyses show it discriminates proficiency levels with very large effects (Hedges' g = 1.34) and correlates r ≈ .66 with other proficiency measures — and a validated Spanish EIT exists.
Yan et al. (2016, Language Testing) meta-analyzed 24 effect sizes (1,089 participants): EI strongly discriminates across proficiency levels (g = 1.34); longer stimuli sentences yield stronger correlations. Kostromitina & Plonsky (2022, SSLA; 60 effect sizes) found EIT scores correlate on average r = .66 with other proficiency measures. The Ortega et al. (2002) Spanish EIT uses 30 sentences of 7-17 syllables scored 0-4 (max 120); Solon et al. (2019, SSLA) revisited it with advanced learners (it separates levels but shows some ceiling compression at high proficiency), and a 2023 study established parallel forms for repeated testing. EI is highly automatable: an external script/spreadsheet can present audio, record repetitions, and an AI can score against the rubric — though repeated use of a single form risks practice effects.
Sources: Yan et al. 2016: https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0265532215594643; Kostromitina & Plonsky 2022: https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1342275; Solon et al. 2019: https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/abs/revisiting-the-spanish-elicited-imitation-task/42A8B42A1971FA73AAA05B8FF3CCCA1C; Parallel forms study: https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S2772766123000307

### LLMs can rate transcribed L2 language against CEFR criteria with moderate-to-good but inconsistent agreement with humans (~.30-.80 across studies); rater bias is real but its direction is contested (leniency in many GPT-4 writing studies, severity in a 2025 synthesis), so calibration and anchoring are essential.
GPT-4 rated short L2 essays on the CEFR scale 'almost as well as modern AWE methods' when given calibration examples, with agreement varying by the writer's L1 (BEA 2023). Multiple studies report GPT-4/ChatGPT leniency and self-preference (higher scores for low-perplexity text) in essay scoring; a TESOL Quarterly study found prompting strategy materially changes alignment. However, a 2025 PRISMA synthesis of 65 studies found LLM-human agreement mostly between .30 and .80 and — contrary to the leniency narrative — reported GPT models scoring more severely than humans under a standardized prompt, indicating bias direction depends on prompt, rubric, and task. For speaking, Bannò et al. (2025, Cambridge ALTA; read in full) applied CEFR analytic descriptors zero-shot (Qwen 2.5 72B) to Whisper transcripts of the Speak & Improve corpus and achieved PCC = 0.761 / SRC = 0.755 with human holistic scores (0.771 with a regression layer), vs 0.821 for a task-fine-tuned speech LLM — competitive, interpretable, but with all phonological-control aspects explicitly excluded because they are unrecoverable from text.
Sources: Rating short L2 essays on CEFR with GPT-4 (BEA 2023): https://aclanthology.org/2023.bea-1.49/; Kim 2025 TESOL Quarterly AES with GPT-4: https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/tesq.3405; Comparing GPT and human raters (2026): https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2666557326000121; Agreement Between LLMs and Human Raters synthesis: https://www.researchgate.net/publication/398766141; Bannò et al. 2025, NLA of L2 oral proficiency using LLMs: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.10200

### A simulated OPI run by an AI can plausibly reproduce the elicitation logic (level checks, probes to breakdown, role-play) because the protocol is publicly documented, but it cannot produce an official rating, and no peer-reviewed validation of a ChatGPT-administered OPI against certified ratings was found.
The OPI's phase structure, assessment criteria, and rating logic are published in the Familiarization Guide, and ACTFL's computer-delivered OPIc demonstrates that semi-automated elicitation can be validly rated. Research on AI oral assessment so far evaluates LLMs as raters of existing speech samples, not as certified interviewers; studies note that transcript-based rating loses prosodic/paralinguistic information. Official ratings require certified testers and blind double-rating (exact agreement on level and sublevel). Practical consequence: an AI-simulated OPI is a useful formative instrument for floor/ceiling estimation if the session prompt encodes the level-check/probe logic and the rating rubric, but external anchoring (an occasional real OPIc via Language Testing International, which individuals can purchase, or a certified tutor evaluation) is needed to keep self-ratings honest.
Sources: ACTFL OPI Familiarization Guide: https://www.actfl.org/uploads/files/general/OPI-Familiarization-Guide-2020-1.pdf; OPIc ACE report: https://www.actfl.org/uploads/files/general/Documents/assessments/acereports/OPIc_Report_Part_A_General_Information_2023.pdf; Assessing oral production with ChatGPT (preprint, abstract only — full text 403): https://www.researchsquare.com/article/rs-9768480/v1

### Pronunciation instruction works for adults — Lee, Jang & Plonsky's meta-analysis of 86 studies found a large mean effect (d = 0.80) — but gains are mostly measured on controlled/read-aloud tasks and as accuracy/accentedness change, and accent is the most change-resistant dimension; comprehensibility, not nativelikeness, is the evidence-based goal.
Lee, Jang & Plonsky (2015) report d = 0.80 versus comparison conditions, with the caveat that most outcome measures were controlled production, not spontaneous speech. Munro & Derwing's foundational work established that intelligibility, comprehensibility, and accentedness are partially independent: heavily accented speech can be fully intelligible, and accentedness correlates only weakly with intelligibility. Longitudinal work finds most naturalistic pronunciation change occurs in an early 'window of maximal opportunity' of massive exposure (Derwing & Munro 2015); adult accentedness after that changes slowly even when comprehensibility and fluency keep improving. Saito's (2021) meta-analyses similarly show instructed gains concentrate in comprehensibility-relevant features. Realistic expectation for this learner: clearly noticeable improvement in comprehensibility, fluency, and specific trained segments; a residual accent is near-certain and pedagogically unimportant.
Sources: Lee, Jang & Plonsky 2015 (via narrative review): https://www.researchgate.net/publication/271827649_The_Effectiveness_of_L2_Pronunciation_Instruction_A_Narrative_Review; Saito 2021 TESOL Quarterly meta-analyses: https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/tesq.3027; Munro & Derwing foundations: https://www.researchgate.net/publication/231904462_The_foundations_of_accent_and_intelligibility_in_pronunciation_research; Derwing & Munro 25-years review: https://www.researchgate.net/publication/346953234

### High Variability Phonetic Training (HVPT) has strong meta-analytic support for improving L2 speech perception (g = 0.92 pre-post; g = 0.67 vs. controls) with durable, partially generalizable gains — but its transfer to production is only small-to-medium (≈10.5% gain on trained items, 4.5% untrained) with weak evidence for retention and generalization of production gains.
Uchihara et al.'s meta-analysis of 79 HVPT studies (Studies in Second Language Acquisition, 2025) confirms medium-to-large perception effects, long-term retention of perception gains, and some generalization to novel stimuli/talkers; moderators include number of talkers, task type, and total training time. A companion meta-analysis of 31 studies on the perception-production link (Applied Psycholinguistics) found only small-to-medium production improvements and no strong support for long-term retention or transfer to untrained items. Design consequence: HVPT-style multi-talker minimal-pair drills are the evidence-based way to build the perceptual categories (e.g., /ɾ/ vs /r/, [d] vs [ð]), but must be paired with explicit articulatory instruction and production practice to move actual pronunciation.
Sources: HVPT perception meta-analysis: https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/high-variability-phonetic-training-hvpt-a-metaanalysis-of-l2-perceptual-training-studies/6ABB8C1F32D88D53EA8D05A4565E76F6; HVPT production meta-analysis: https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/applied-psycholinguistics/article/does-perceptual-high-variability-phonetic-training-improve-l2-speech-production-a-metaanalysis-of-perceptionproduction-connection/E38D8F5CE65DC708137B0E95F97C6BC7

### Explicit phonetics instruction demonstrably improves English speakers' production and perception of the specific Spanish problem sounds: unaspirated /p t k/, the spirant approximants [β ð ɣ] (written b/v, d, g), the tap-trill contrast /ɾ/-/r/, and maintaining full unreduced vowels in Spanish's five-vowel system; shadowing adds prosody/fluency benefits.
Kissling (2013, Modern Language Journal; n = 95) targeted exactly these segments in L2 Spanish learners and found both explicit phonetics instruction and matched implicit practice improved production accuracy; Kissling (2015) found phonetics instruction conferred a (small, delayed-posttest) perception advantage across course levels; Lord (2005) found a Spanish phonetics course improved production. The linguistic analysis behind these targets: English's only rhotic (alveolar approximant) exists in no Spanish variety, which uses tap vs trill; English speakers must also suppress vowel reduction/schwa (Spanish has 5 stable vowels, no length distinction, minimal spectral reduction in unstressed syllables) and learn intervocalic spirantization of /b d g/. An Oxford systematic review of shadowing finds it improves comprehensibility, accentedness ratings, and prosodic control (rhythm, intonation, pitch), while effects on individual segments are inconclusive. Intonation-specific L2 Spanish training research is comparatively thin.
Sources: Kissling 2013: https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1540-4781.2013.12029.x; Kissling 2015 perception: https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/1362168814541735; Lord 2005: https://www.researchgate.net/publication/271746523; Shadowing systematic review (Oxford ORA): https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid:3104cae1-3a6b-400a-b173-de44384238d2; Spanish rhotics for L2 learners: http://www.lingref.com/cpp/casp/7/paper1274.pdf; Vowel reduction in Spanish-English bilinguals: https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5383264/

### Current LLM voice models cannot reliably judge pronunciation at the phonetic level: GPT-4o performed at or below the 33% random baseline on multilingual pronunciation-similarity judgments, refuses or fails many phonological tasks, and its pronunciation scores correlate far worse with human raters (PCC ≈ 0.28-0.58) than dedicated ASR-based systems (PCC ≈ 0.69-0.81).
The 'Preliminary Exploration with GPT-4o Voice Mode' benchmark (arXiv 2502.09940) found no large audio-language model beat the random baseline on triplet pronunciation-similarity classification, and GPT-4o showed high refusal/failure rates on phonological feature classification and phoneme counting — though it is relatively better at coarse tasks (accent/prosody/fluency classification) conveyed by audio. A dedicated evaluation (arXiv 2503.11229) on Speechocean762 measured GPT-4o audio at sentence-level PCC 0.445, word-level 0.277, phoneme-level 0.575 — versus 0.811/0.694 for a state-of-the-art dedicated scorer — with 41-48% incomplete-scoring failures; the authors conclude LMMs cannot replace dedicated pronunciation assessment, though GPT-4o generating verbal feedback FROM a dedicated system's scores was rated helpful (hybrid approach). Anecdotally, voice-mode chat also tends to 'hear' learner speech through ASR normalization, which silently corrects mispronunciations. Implication: do not task GPT-Live-1 with segmental accent scoring; it can reasonably flag gross mispronunciations, unnatural prosody, or comprehensibility breakdowns.
Sources: GPT-4o Voice Mode exploration: https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.09940 (HTML: https://arxiv.org/html/2502.09940v1); LMMs for pronunciation assessment: https://arxiv.org/html/2503.11229v1

### Dedicated ASR-based pronunciation scoring (Azure Pronunciation Assessment, Speechace, ELSA-style CAPT) is the practical accent-feedback channel, and Azure supports Spanish (es-ES and es-MX) with phoneme-level scores — but even these tools correlate only moderately with expert human raters, so scores should drive practice selection, not serve as ground truth.
Azure Pronunciation Assessment is generally available for Spanish with phoneme-, word-, and sentence-level accuracy/fluency/prosody scores, and Microsoft validates it via Pearson correlation with human judges; independent studies find moderate agreement (e.g., an Arabic clinical study: Azure-expert PCC 0.53-0.67 vs. human-human 0.86-0.93; a Korean learner study found Azure phoneme scores moderately correlated with human judgments while Kaldi GOP was uncorrelated). CAPT intervention studies (SpeechAce, ELSA) show real pronunciation gains for learners using phoneme-level feedback, though study designs are often weak (no spontaneous-speech outcome measures). Note ELSA targets English learners; for Spanish the practical options are apps built on Azure/Speechace-class engines, or direct use of Azure's free pronunciation-assessment tool in Speech Studio. Microsoft's own documentation lists limitations (accent sensitivity, noise, short-utterance instability).
Sources: Azure PA language support (Spanish es-ES/es-MX): https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/ai-services/speech-service/language-support?tabs=pronunciation-assessment; Azure PA characteristics and limitations: https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/foundry/responsible-ai/speech-service/pronunciation-assessment/characteristics-and-limitations-pronunciation-assessment; Word error rate as pronunciation proxy / ASR comparison: https://www.jbe-platform.com/content/journals/10.1075/jslp.25012.won; Arabic phoneme-level framework with Azure comparison: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.06191; SpeechAce EFL study: https://www.researchgate.net/publication/391669599

### Small experimental studies report measurable speaking gains from ChatGPT voice-mode conversation practice (statistically significant pre/post improvements, biggest in fluency and vocabulary), but the evidence base is young, mostly short-duration EFL studies with weak controls — nothing yet at the 500-hour scale or for Spanish specifically.
A 2025 EFL study using ChatGPT Voice found post-test speaking scores ~3 points higher (p < .001), with fluency (+2.8) and vocabulary (+2.7) gaining most, and 85% of participants crediting voice mode for accent exposure and feedback; review papers catalogue potentials (low anxiety, unlimited practice, personalization) and concerns (ASR normalization of learner errors, over-accommodating interlocutor behavior, no principled curriculum). No RCTs against human tutoring at scale exist. This supports using AI conversation as the practice engine while remaining skeptical that the AI's in-conversation impressions constitute valid proficiency measurement.
Sources: Using ChatGPT Voice to improve speaking (EFL): https://www.researchgate.net/publication/389801877; Speaking practice with ChatGPT voice — review: https://www.researchgate.net/publication/383471345; AI chatbot ESL speaking pathway study: https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2590291125006618

### A credible solo assessment architecture is a triangulated battery on a fixed cycle: scripted simulated OPI (floor/ceiling estimate), Spanish elicited imitation test (objective anchor), timed monologues re-rated later against ACTFL/CEFR speaking descriptors, writing samples rated with calibration exemplars, Can-Do inventories for curriculum tracking, and a periodic external human anchor.
Each component covers another's weakness: EI is objective and sensitive but narrow; the simulated OPI covers interactive functions but depends on a biased AI rater; LLM rating of transcripts/writing is competitive with trained raters when given calibrated exemplars and explicit CEFR analytic descriptors (Bannò et al. 2025 achieved r≈0.76 zero-shot with exactly this descriptor-based method) but cannot see phonology; Can-Dos add curricular coverage but inflate at high sublevels; an occasional official OPIc or certified-tutor rating recalibrates the whole system against drift. Recording every assessment (audio + transcript) externally is essential both because ChatGPT sessions start fresh (no cross-session memory) and because longitudinal re-rating of old samples with a fixed rubric is the cheapest defensible progress measure.
Sources: Bannò et al. 2025: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.10200; Yan et al. 2016 EI meta-analysis: https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0265532215594643; OPI Familiarization Guide: https://www.actfl.org/uploads/files/general/OPI-Familiarization-Guide-2020-1.pdf; Li & Zhang 2021 self-assessment meta-analysis: https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/0265532220932481

## Design implications
- Baseline in week 1 with a triangulated battery, not an assumption: run the Ortega et al. 30-item Spanish EIT (audio presented from an external script, responses recorded), a scripted simulated OPI using the published four-phase protocol, and the NCSSFL-ACTFL Intermediate/Advanced speaking Can-Dos. Working hypothesis to verify: speaking floor at Intermediate Low-Mid, listening/reading near Intermediate High/B1+.
- Set the 500-hour target at ACTFL Advanced Mid speaking (≈ CEFR B2.2 by ACTFL's official one-directional crosswalk), with Superior-level functions (supported opinion, hypothesis, abstract discussion) as stretch probes in the final phase. FSI/ETS hour tables make this ambitious-but-plausible for a Category I language with savings effects from prior IH-level attainment.
- Engineer the simulated OPI as a reusable prompt artifact: encode warm-up → level checks → probes-to-breakdown → role-play → wind-down, the four assessment criteria (function, context/content, accuracy, text type), and the floor/ceiling rating logic. Run it monthly-to-quarterly, always recording audio and saving transcripts externally, since each ChatGPT session starts fresh.
- Counter LLM rater bias structurally: paste ACTFL/CEFR level descriptors and 2-3 calibration exemplars into every rating session; have the model rate analytic aspects separately (grammatical accuracy, vocabulary range, fluency, coherence) and aggregate, per the Bannò et al. descriptor-based method; occasionally re-submit an old recording blind to detect drift; and treat single-session ratings as ±1 sublevel noise. Do not let the conversation model rate the conversation it just participated in — use a separate rating session on the transcript/recording.
- Buy external anchors sparingly but deliberately: an official ACTFL OPIc through Language Testing International (individuals can purchase) or a certified-examiner mock DELE/SIELE at roughly hours 0/250/500 gives the only rater-drift-proof calibration of the home-grown battery; DELE/SIELE B2-C1 are the CEFR-native alternatives.
- Do not assign segmental pronunciation scoring to the voice model: GPT-4o-class models are at chance on pronunciation-similarity judgment and far below dedicated systems in phoneme/word-level correlation with humans. Restrict the voice model to comprehensibility-level feedback (did it impede understanding, gross prosody, register) and explicit-knowledge coaching (articulatory explanations, minimal-pair word lists).
- Build a separate pronunciation loop: (a) HVPT-style multi-talker perception drills on the four problem areas — tap/trill, [β ð ɣ] approximants, unaspirated /p t k/, full unreduced vowels — using varied native audio (multiple speakers/dialects); (b) explicit phonetics mini-lessons (Kissling/Lord-style), which the text/voice model CAN deliver well; (c) shadowing of native audio with self-recording and A/B comparison for prosody; (d) an ASR-based phoneme scorer that supports Spanish — Azure Pronunciation Assessment (es-MX) via Speech Studio or an app built on it — treating its scores as practice-targeting signals, not ground truth.
- Set accent expectations by the intelligibility principle: track comprehensibility and fluency (e.g., periodic timed monologues judged for effort-to-understand, words-per-minute, pause patterns) rather than 'accent reduction'; expect trained-segment improvement and prosody gains, and accept a residual accent as normal and communicatively irrelevant.
- Exploit the attrition profile: because receptive knowledge and proceduralized grammar likely survived while lexical access and productive fluency decayed, front-load the first ~50-100 hours with high-volume reactivation (extensive listening/reading, retrieval-based vocabulary work, fluency-focused speaking) rather than re-teaching grammar from scratch; expect fast early gains and design the level-check schedule to capture them (more frequent assessment early).
- Because cross-session memory must live outside ChatGPT, maintain an external curriculum state file: current working level, Can-Do checklist status, error log (recurring grammar/vocabulary issues harvested from session transcripts), EIT and monologue scores over time, and the next session's targets — pasted or summarized into each session's opening prompt.
- Use elicited imitation as the recurring objective dipstick (it is the psychometrically strongest 10-minute measure available to a solo learner), but rotate parallel forms or build new sentence sets periodically to avoid practice effects, and keep scoring criteria fixed (0-4 per item, meaning-preserving verbatim rubric).
- Add writing to the assessment mix deliberately: CEFR-rubric-scored writing samples are where LLM rating evidence is strongest (near-AWE agreement with calibration), making writing the cheapest reliably-measured skill; use it to triangulate grammar control that speaking assessments measure noisily.

## Open questions
- No validated College Board-ACTFL concordance study for AP world-language exam scores exists (ACTFL's own mapping is labeled 'anecdotal evidence and examination of the scoring rubric') — the learner's true baseline needs direct measurement, not inference from the 15-year-old AP score.
- No peer-reviewed study yet validates an LLM-administered simulated OPI against certified human OPI ratings — the elicitation protocol is replicable on paper, but whether an AI interviewer probes to genuine breakdown (rather than accommodating the learner) is untested.
- The direction and size of LLM rater bias (lenient vs. severe) varies across studies, prompts, and models, and almost all evidence is for English L2; how a 2026-era voice model rates Spanish speech transcripts against ACTFL descriptors is essentially unstudied.
- GPT-Live-1 itself has no published benchmark data on phonetic judgment; the strong negative evidence is for GPT-4o-class models, so a brief self-test (deliberately mispronouncing minimal pairs and seeing whether the model detects it) would be worth running before trusting or dismissing its pronunciation feedback.
- Whether ASR-based pronunciation scores for Spanish (validated mostly on English read-aloud speech) remain moderately human-correlated on spontaneous L2-Spanish speech is unclear; most validity evidence is for scripted reading tasks.
- How self-directed AI-conversation hours convert to FSI-classroom-hour equivalents is unknown — the 480/720-hour ACTFL benchmarks assume intensive small-group instruction, so the 500-hour target's feasibility band is wide.
- Long-run effects of conversing predominantly with a synthetic interlocutor on accent and pragmatics (accommodation to TTS prosody, absence of dialectal variability) have not been studied; mixing in human interaction and multi-speaker listening is a hedge, not an evidence-backed prescription.
- The Spanish EIT's ceiling behavior at Advanced+ levels (Solon et al. 2019 found compression among advanced learners) means the program may need a harder progress measure for the final 200 hours.
