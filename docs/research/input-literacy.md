# input-literacy

## Summary
The literatures on listening, reading, and writing as learning activities are individually robust enough to justify treating each as a core strand — not an assessment afterthought — in a conversation-centered plan, and the strongest single message is that skills are substantially (though not totally) specific: conversation practice alone will not build the ability to decode fast colloquial Mexican Spanish, and input alone will not build speaking. For listening, the best-supported interventions for a false-intermediate are (a) explicit bottom-up decoding training — dictation/transcription of short stretches of connected speech — where a 2021 systematic review found a meta-analytic advantage of Hedges' g = 0.55 over non-decoding instruction, and (b) captioned L2 video, where meta-analyses find large effects on both listening comprehension (g ≈ 0.99) and vocabulary (g ≈ 0.87) versus no captions. Slowed audio is a legitimate scaffold with surprisingly good evidence: in McBride (2011), learners trained on slowed recordings improved bottom-up processing most and transferred best to normal-rate speech. The Spanish-specific decoding problem for this learner is less s-aspiration (marginal in most of Mexico) than resyllabification/synalepha across word boundaries and central-Mexican unstressed vowel reduction ("pues"→"ps", "para"→"pa'"), which are documented obstacles even for advanced L2 listeners. Extensive listening research is enthusiastic but methodologically weaker (few controlled studies; effect estimates for generic "listening instruction" run small, d ≈ 0.25), so volume claims like Dreaming Spanish's 1,000–1,500-hour roadmap should be treated as practitioner heuristics, not findings.

For reading, two meta-analyses (Nakanishi 2015: d = 0.46 group contrasts, 0.71 pre–post; Jeon & Day 2016: small-to-medium effects, larger for adults) support extensive reading, with important caveats: time-on-task confounds, overrepresentation of university EFL samples, tentative moderator findings, and published critiques of construct definitions. Reading's clearest unique contribution is vocabulary breadth and depth (collocations, register) at rates estimated around 8 words per contact hour versus ~2 for instruction, plus consolidation of grammar in context. The 98% (reading) / 95% (listening) lexical-coverage guidelines — from Hu & Nation (2000), Schmitt et al. (2011), van Zeeland & Schmitt (2013), with a 2023 registered replication supporting a strong coverage–comprehension link but a probabilistic rather than hard threshold — give a principled basis for a progression: B1/B2 graded readers (Olly Richards, Paco Ardit) → news → adapted then unmodified fiction, with reading-while-listening as a well-evidenced bridge activity that also grows listening fluency.

For writing, the Truscott-vs-Ferris/Bitchener written corrective feedback (WCF) debate has largely resolved in favor of moderate positive effects on new-writing accuracy: Kang & Han (2015) and Lim & Renandya (2020; 35 studies, g = 0.59, delayed-posttest g = 0.57) both find WCF works, with no reliable superiority of direct over indirect or focused over comprehensive feedback (though indirect looked better at delayed posttest, and Ferris's treatable/untreatable distinction argues for explicit rules on rule-governed errors — exactly the fossilized ser/estar, preterite/imperfect, subjunctive territory of a rusty AP-4 learner). Truscott's residual objections — that gains reflect avoidance or explicit-only knowledge — remain partially unanswered and worth monitoring via complexity measures. Writing + LLM correction is a uniquely strong pairing for this plan for a mechanical reason: ASR systems (including Whisper-class models) show elevated error rates and error-normalization on non-native speech, so a voice tutor literally cannot see many of the learner's errors, whereas text preserves the exact interlanguage. Early empirical work on ChatGPT WCF finds it accurate, patient, and sympathetic but biased toward rewriting whole sentences instead of eliciting self-correction — a prompt-engineering problem, not a capability ceiling. On the four-skills balance: declarative knowledge (words, rules) transfers across skills, but proceduralization is skill-specific — 37 hours of input-based instruction improved listening but not speaking in one study — so reading/listening/writing should be framed as feeding the conversation sessions (vocabulary, decoding, accuracy) rather than substituting for them, and vice versa.

## Findings
### Explicit bottom-up decoding training (dictation/transcription) has solid meta-analytic support for L2 listening
A 2021 systematic review in Educational Research Review of EFL/ESL decoding training meta-analyzed 13 intervention studies and found decoding training beat non-decoding instruction with Hedges' g = 0.553 (95% CI 0.348–0.759), with no publication-bias evidence. The most common training tasks were dictation (15 studies) and pronunciation instruction (10), followed by imitation, noticing-with-support, discrimination tasks, and shadowing. This operationalizes John Field's long-standing argument (Listening in the Language Classroom; his 2008 TESOL Quarterly work) that L2 listeners' failures are mostly failures of lexical segmentation and decoding of connected speech, best fixed by short targeted transcription of natural speech rather than comprehension-question practice.
Sources: https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1747938X21000154; https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/j.1545-7249.2008.tb00139.x; https://gianfrancoconti.com/2025/03/19/transforming-l2-listening-instruction-powerful-insights-from-prof-john-field-the-leading-expert-in-the-field/

### Metacognitive strategy instruction (Vandergrift & Goh) helps, but combining it with bottom-up training beats either alone for weaker listeners
Dalman & Plonsky's meta-analysis of 45 listening strategy-instruction studies found a medium effect (d = 0.69); a separate 2024 meta-analysis of listening instruction generally found only d = 0.25, suggesting typical 'practice listening + questions' instruction is weak. An IRAL experimental comparison found the integrated metacognitive + bottom-up condition most effective for low-proficiency listeners, with bottom-up-only second. Consensus position: decoding is the binding constraint for intermediate listeners; strategies are a complement, not a substitute.
Sources: https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/13621688211072981; https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/10904018.2022.2074851; https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1515/iral-2023-0142/pdf?lang=en

### Slowed audio during training is better supported than its reputation suggests — but moderate slowing only, and evidence is mixed
McBride (2011, CALL) trained four groups (fast recordings, slow recordings, learner-choice speed, pausable playback); the slow-trained group fared best on transfer, apparently because bottom-up processing improved, and distributed practice across days helped. Zhao (1997, Applied Linguistics) found learner control over speech rate improved comprehension. But effects are non-linear: one study found benefit at a mildly slowed rate with no additional benefit at extremely slow rates, and slowing helps most with heavily accented/difficult input. Medina (2020, MLJ) confirmed speech rate and utterance length independently predict L2 Spanish listening difficulty. Caveat: artificially slowed audio doesn't contain the connected-speech reductions of real fast speech, so it must be a scaffold en route to native rate, not a destination — the overall literature on rate manipulation remains genuinely mixed.
Sources: https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/09588221.2010.528777; https://academic.oup.com/applij/article-abstract/18/1/49/233475; https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/modl.12639; https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/9781118784235.eelt0576

### The Spanish-specific decoding targets for a Mexico-focused learner: resyllabification/synalepha, and central-Mexican unstressed vowel reduction — s-aspiration is mostly a non-Mexican dialect issue
Spanish resyllabifies word-final consonants onto following vowels ('mis amigos' → mi.sa.mi.gos) and links vowels across word boundaries (synalepha), which is a documented parsing obstacle: even advanced English-speaking learners retain a bias toward hearing consonant-initial words, though it lessens with proficiency. Central Mexican (highland) Spanish is conservative with /s/ but shows heavy unstressed vowel reduction/devoicing next to /s/ in fast speech ('pescado' → 'pscado', 'pues' → 'ps'), plus lexicalized reductions ('para' → 'pa''). S-aspiration ([h] for coda /s/) is characteristic of Caribbean/coastal and Rioplatense varieties rather than most of Mexico; studies show less-proficient learners misparse aspirated /s/ (e.g., as /f/). Implication: train linking/resyllabification and vowel-reduced casual Mexican speech deliberately; treat aspirating dialects as a later, separate module.
Sources: https://www.researchgate.net/publication/303382920_On_the_acquisition_of_synalepha_and_resyllabification_in_Spanish_by_English-speaking_advanced_learners; https://www.ideals.illinois.edu/items/102296; https://en-academic.com/dic.nsf/enwiki/745210; https://www.fon.hum.uva.nl/archive/2022/2022-BA-PaulaPels.pdf; https://www.open.edu/openlearn/mod/oucontent/view.php?id=105251&section=5

### Captioned L2 video produces large gains in listening and vocabulary; L2 captions generally beat L1 subtitles for language learning
Montero Perez et al.'s (2013) meta-analysis found captioned (L2 text) beat uncaptioned video with g = 0.99 for listening comprehension (15 studies) and g = 0.87 for vocabulary (10 studies); proficiency did not moderate effects. Kurokawa et al. (2025, Language Learning, 75(4)) updated this for incidental vocabulary specifically, confirming captioning advantages with moderator analyses. Montero Perez's (2022) Language Teaching review and a 2022 Frontiers review conclude L2 captions support form learning and speech segmentation in ways L1 subtitles cannot; L1-subtitle results are mixed, and input difficulty appropriateness matters more than caption type. Bilingual subtitles show promise for meaning recall. Caveat: captions raise measured 'listening' scores partly by letting learners read; caption-free viewing passes are needed to force auditory decoding.
Sources: https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0346251X13001012; https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/lang.12697; https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/language-teaching/article/abs/second-or-foreign-language-learning-through-watching-audiovisual-input-and-the-role-of-onscreen-text/7CBDDEC3187825A0776C521C6842A75B; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9136233/

### Extensive listening/viewing: strong theoretical case and practitioner enthusiasm, weaker controlled evidence; reading-while-listening is the best-evidenced variant
Ivone & Renandya's reviews argue extensive listening builds listening fluency and word-recognition speed, but the evidence base is dominated by small classroom studies rather than RCTs. The most convincing longitudinal data comes from reading-while-listening: Chang's 26-week audiobook RWL intervention produced ~566 words gained vs 123 in controls and >100% dictation-score improvement; Hui (2024, MLJ) shows RWL scaffolds comprehension with benefits depending on speed/text complexity. Supported listening (transcripts available) outperforms unsupported listening for sound-form mapping. For volume: no experimental literature pins down required hours; Dreaming Spanish's roadmap (600–1,000 hrs = comfortable with daily conversation; 1,500 hrs = 'effective user') is explicitly a practitioner estimate, not research.
Sources: https://www.researchgate.net/publication/347341889_EXTENSIVE_LISTENING_AND_VIEWING_IN_ELT; https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/The-Effect-of-Reading-While-Listening-to-Listening-Chang/ea90e52b1c44d096273546e1cdd30d71de815b9b; https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/modl.12905; https://www.dreaming.com/faq; https://www.scribd.com/document/793209760/Language-Learning-Roadmap-by-Dreaming-Spanish

### Intermediate Spanish listening resources exist in a usable difficulty ladder, including Mexican-specific material
Dreaming Spanish offers 6,500+ videos graded superbeginner→advanced with 1–100 difficulty ratings (comprehensible-input method, Krashen-inspired). For Mexican Spanish specifically, No Hay Tos is an intermediate/advanced podcast by two Mexican teachers speaking at natural speed with slang, with transcripts via newsletter/Patreon — well matched to the learner's dialect target. Radio Ambulante (NPR) provides journalist-quality pan-Latin-American narrative audio with full Spanish/English transcripts, appropriate at upper-intermediate/advanced. This supports a progression: Dreaming Spanish intermediate/advanced → No Hay Tos → Radio Ambulante/native media.
Sources: https://www.dreaming.com/spanish; https://www.nohaytospodcast.com/; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radio_Ambulante; https://spanishmama.com/the-best-spanish-podcasts-for-learning-spanish/

### Extensive reading meta-analyses show moderate positive effects — with real caveats
Nakanishi (2015, TESOL Quarterly; 34 studies, 3,942 participants) found d = 0.46 for group contrasts and d = 0.71 pre–post. Jeon & Day (2016; 71 samples, 5,919 participants) found small-to-medium effects on reading proficiency, larger for adults than children — encouraging for this learner. A 2025 Educational Psychology Review meta-analysis confirms positive effects across L2 outcomes. Caveats acknowledged in the literature: pre–post designs inflate estimates, time-on-task is rarely controlled (ER groups often simply read more), university EFL samples are overrepresented, moderator findings are tentative, and published critiques (e.g., of construct definition in Nakanishi) argue 'extensive reading' is operationalized inconsistently across studies.
Sources: https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/tesq.157; https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/The-effectiveness-of-ER-on-reading-proficiency:-A-Jeon-Day/91e92465a118ac02ca67f6c7b043607a044ae53d; https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-025-10068-6

### Lexical coverage research gives a principled basis for choosing graded vs authentic texts: ~98% known-word coverage for comfortable reading, ~95% for listening
Hu & Nation (2000) found 98% coverage needed for adequate unassisted comprehension of fiction; Schmitt et al. (2011) and Laufer & Ravenhorst-Kalovski (2010) support 95% = minimal, 98% = optimal. Van Zeeland & Schmitt (2013) found listening more tolerant (~95%, with adequate comprehension even at 90% for some listeners). Kremmel et al.'s (2023, Language Learning) registered replication of Hu & Nation confirmed the strong coverage–comprehension relationship while showing it is gradient/probabilistic rather than a sharp threshold. Practical consequence for a B1-ish learner: unmodified novels and rapid-fire news will sit below comfortable coverage; graded readers (Olly Richards B1–B2 Short Stories, Paco Ardit Spanish Novels series) and adapted texts are the efficient zone, with the documented progression parallel text → graded reader → adapted native → unmodified native (news before literary fiction, since news vocabulary is more frequent/repetitive).
Sources: https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/lang.12622; https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1316858.pdf; https://trivialingua.com/blog/best-spanish-graded-readers; https://eppika.com/en/blog/how-to-learn-spanish-through-reading; https://www.goodreads.com/en/book/show/45032245

### Reading's distinctive contribution is vocabulary depth (collocations, nuance, register) and grammar consolidation at high uptake rates
Extensive reading improves both vocabulary size and quality (nuances, collocations, usage) through repeated contextual encounters; one estimate from the ER literature puts uptake around 8 words per contact hour of ER vs ~2 words per hour of instruction. Mode-of-input research (SSLA) shows reading is at least as effective as listening for incidental collocation learning. This addresses the specific weakness of a conversation-centered plan: conversation recycles the learner's existing (fossilized, high-frequency) lexicon, while reading systematically feeds mid-frequency vocabulary and formulaic sequences the learner will later deploy in speech.
Sources: https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1219495.pdf; https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1465146.pdf; https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/abs/how-does-mode-of-input-affect-the-incidental-learning-of-collocations/CCA20D5A9DF56046DCB84648DCFAC554; https://www2.hawaii.edu/~readfl/rfl/October2008/kweon/kweon.html

### Written corrective feedback works: the Truscott debate has substantially resolved toward moderate positive effects, though his deepest objection is not fully answered
Truscott (1996, and since) argued error correction is ineffective/harmful and produces only 'pseudo-learning' (explicit, not acquired knowledge). Against this: Bitchener & Knoch's 10-month study (Applied Linguistics 2010) showed durable gains from focused WCF on English articles; Kang & Han's (2015, MLJ) meta-analysis of 21 studies found WCF improves grammatical accuracy, moderated by proficiency/setting/genre; Lim & Renandya (2020, TESL-EJ; 35 studies) found overall g = 0.59 (CI 0.42–0.76) with delayed-posttest g = 0.57, indicating retention. Truscott's remaining substantive point — that many outcome measures are revisions or accuracy-only (possibly reflecting avoidance of complex forms), and effects on genuinely new linguistic knowledge are weaker — is acknowledged in the field and argues for measuring progress on new texts and tracking complexity alongside accuracy. Note also both meta-analyses attracted published methodological critiques, so the g ≈ 0.6 figure has error bars in practice.
Sources: https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/modl.12189; https://tesl-ej.org/wordpress/issues/volume24/ej95/ej95a3/; https://academic.oup.com/applij/article-abstract/31/2/193/177926; https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40862-021-00110-9; https://www.researchgate.net/publication/341453398_The_Efficacy_of_Written_Corrective_Feedback_A_Critique_of_a_Meta-analysis

### Which WCF type: no robust winner between direct/indirect or focused/comprehensive — but proficiency moderates, indirect may aid retention, and Ferris's treatable/untreatable distinction is the most actionable guidance
Lim & Renandya (2020): direct g = 0.761 vs indirect g = 0.625 (n.s.); focused g = 0.628 vs unfocused g = 0.445 (n.s.); at delayed posttest indirect (g = 0.998) outperformed direct (g = 0.531) on limited data; effects were strongest for low/low-intermediate learners (g = 0.982). Kang & Han similarly found no significant focused-vs-unfocused difference. Ferris (1999, 2003): 'treatable' rule-governed errors (agreement, tense, articles — in Spanish: ser/estar, preterite/imperfect, gender/number agreement, subjunctive triggers) respond to indirect coded feedback + rule reference, while idiosyncratic 'untreatable' errors (word choice, idiom) need direct correction. For a self-directed learner with fossilized errors, this supports cycles of focused feedback on 1–2 treatable error families at a time, with indirect/metalinguistic prompting before reveal of the correction.
Sources: https://tesl-ej.org/wordpress/issues/volume24/ej95/ej95a3/; https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1075180.pdf; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7829097/

### Writing + LLM correction is a uniquely strong pairing for this plan — for a mechanical ASR reason and an emerging empirical one — but default LLM feedback style needs prompt engineering
ASR systems show systematically higher error rates on non-native-accented speech (mainstream systems are trained on native corpora; Whisper-class models approach human accuracy on read non-native speech but degrade on spontaneous speech and clinical/real contexts), and ASR tends to normalize or miss precisely the learner errors a tutor would need to hear — so a voice-only tutor is structurally blind to much of the learner's interlanguage, while text preserves it exactly. On the LLM side: ElEbyary & Shabara (2024, Teaching English with Technology) found ChatGPT's WCF on B1 essays was accurate, spotted surface errors teachers missed, was more supportive in tone, and 75% of its comments were lexically within learners' comprehension range — but it habitually rewrote whole sentences despite instructions to give feedback only, which short-circuits the noticing/self-correction mechanism WCF theory depends on; teachers were better at precise direct error-focused feedback. A Nature Humanities & Social Sciences Communications case study found learner engagement with ChatGPT AWCF varies widely by individual. Implication: script the LLM into staged feedback (locate → code → metalinguistic hint → learner self-corrects → reveal), rather than accepting its default rewrite behavior.
Sources: https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.06924; https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0167639324000104; https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1460051.pdf; https://www.nature.com/articles/s41599-024-03543-y; https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0346251X25002155

### Output-based writing tasks (dictogloss, text reconstruction, translation/retranslation) have supporting but thinner evidence, working through Swain's noticing/hypothesis-testing functions
Swain's output hypothesis holds that production forces syntactic processing that comprehension does not, driving noticing of gaps — the theoretical reason writing can attack fossilization that input alone leaves intact (her evidence: French immersion students with excellent comprehension but persistently inaccurate production). Dictogloss research shows it promotes noticing and grammar/formulaic-sequence learning, with a key moderator: items learners successfully retrieved during reconstruction were retained best (Yu, Boers & Tremblay 2025, Language Teaching Research); advantages over comprehension tasks shrank at delayed posttest in some studies. Translation exercises: classroom studies and an ELT Journal report support translation (including translating one's own writing) for objectifying grammar differences and improving accuracy, but this literature is mostly small-scale and pre-experimental — no meta-analysis exists comparable to the WCF or ER literatures. Treat these as plausible, theory-consistent techniques rather than proven ones.
Sources: https://dergipark.org.tr/en/pub/eku/issue/55567/737595; https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/13621688221117242; https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0889490615000447; https://academic.oup.com/eltj/article/65/2/154/527580

### Four-skills transfer: declarative knowledge transfers, procedural skill largely does not — input-only practice demonstrably fails to build speaking, and speaking-only practice will not build fast-speech listening
DeKeyser's skill acquisition theory predicts practice effects are skill-specific because proceduralization is tied to the practiced behavior (transfer-appropriate processing). Direct evidence: a study giving 37 hours of input-based instruction over 3 months found significant listening gains but little/no speaking improvement, contradicting strong comprehension-first claims; conversely, reviews note listening and speaking are 'asymmetric in procedural knowledge but symmetric in declarative knowledge' — vocabulary and grammar learned receptively do become available for production after production practice. The counter-tradition (comprehension-first approaches; some studies claiming rapid listening→speaking transfer) exists but is weaker empirically. Practical reading: reading/listening/writing are high-value because they feed the declarative base (vocab breadth/depth, decoded phonological forms, de-fossilized grammar) that conversation sessions then proceduralize — but none substitutes for the conversation hours, and the conversation hours don't substitute for them.
Sources: https://www.academia.edu/34878382/Skill_specificity_of_language_practice_and_transferability_of_language_skills_The_case_of_listening_and_speaking; https://www.researchgate.net/publication/285409570_Skill_acquisition_theory_and_the_role_of_practice_in_L2_development; https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1066407.pdf; https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2022.836013/full

## Design implications
- Add a dedicated 10-15 min/day bottom-up decoding strand: micro-dictations/partial transcriptions of 20-60 second clips of natural-speed Mexican Spanish (No Hay Tos, Mexican YouTube/TV), checked against transcripts — this is the single best-evidenced listening intervention (g ≈ 0.55) and directly targets the connected-speech parsing a voice-AI conversation never trains (the AI speaks clearly and adapts to the learner).
- Explicitly train Spanish resyllabification/linking and central-Mexican vowel reduction as decoding targets (mi.sa.mi.gos parsing, 'ps', 'pa'', devoiced vowels near /s/); deprioritize s-aspiration until adding Caribbean/Rioplatense listening later.
- Use L2 Spanish captions (not English subtitles) as the default for video; schedule repeat or alternate viewing without captions to force auditory decoding, since captioned gains partly reflect reading; match video difficulty to ~95% coverage.
- Budget substantial extensive listening/viewing volume outside the ~1-hour conversation sessions (podcast ladder: Dreaming Spanish intermediate/advanced → No Hay Tos → Radio Ambulante → native media), treating hour-count roadmaps as heuristics; use transcripts for support and reading-while-listening (audiobook + text) as a bridge activity with strong longitudinal evidence.
- Slowed audio (e.g., 0.8x on authentic podcasts) is a legitimate scaffold — McBride's slow-trained group transferred best — but only moderate slowing, always graduating back to 1.0x, and never as the terminal condition since slowed speech lacks real reductions.
- Build an extensive reading strand at ~98% coverage: B1-B2 graded readers (Olly Richards, Paco Ardit) → news (more frequent vocabulary) → adapted fiction → unmodified fiction; position it as the primary engine of mid-frequency vocabulary breadth/depth and collocation learning that conversation cannot supply.
- Make daily short writing + LLM feedback the de-fossilization channel: text preserves the learner's exact errors (no ASR distortion), and WCF meta-analytic evidence (g ≈ 0.59, retained at delay) is stronger than for almost any other accuracy intervention; run focused cycles on 1-2 treatable Spanish error families at a time (ser/estar, preterite/imperfect, gender agreement, subjunctive), measured on new texts, not revisions.
- Prompt-engineer the LLM's feedback protocol against its default behavior: staged feedback (locate error → error code → metalinguistic hint → learner self-corrects → reveal correct form), because studies show ChatGPT otherwise rewrites whole sentences, bypassing the noticing/self-correction mechanism that makes WCF work; track error-rate-per-100-words longitudinally in the external curriculum files.
- Include dictogloss/text-reconstruction and retranslation of the learner's own texts as periodic writing tasks (theory-consistent, moderately evidenced); design them so the learner retrieves before checking, since retrieved items are retained best.
- Frame the four-skills split around skill-specificity: reading/listening/writing feed the declarative base and decoding ability, conversation sessions proceduralize it — so protect the non-speaking strands from being cannibalized (a plausible research-informed allocation for a conversation-goal learner is roughly half the total hours on listening+reading+writing), and pipe vocabulary/structures from reading and corrected writing into the next conversation session's agenda via the external memory files.

## Open questions
- No experimental studies yet test whether accuracy gains from LLM-provided written corrective feedback transfer to spontaneous speech accuracy — the core bet of pairing daily writing with voice conversation sessions is theoretically motivated (declarative→procedural) but empirically untested.
- The optimal ratio of the four skills for a conversation-goal learner has no direct empirical answer; skill-specificity research says all strands are needed but not in what proportion at what stage.
- Nearly all of the listening-decoding, WCF, and extensive-reading evidence comes from English-as-L2 classroom populations; effect sizes for L1-English adults learning Spanish self-directed are extrapolations.
- Whether training on slowed audio outperforms training on native-rate audio with transcript support (the two main scaffolds) has not been directly compared; McBride's design did not include a transcript-support arm.
- Truscott's residual objection stands: most WCF studies measure short-term accuracy on familiar task types; whether WCF-driven gains reflect acquisition versus monitored explicit knowledge plus avoidance (reduced complexity) needs the plan to track complexity alongside accuracy.
- How accurate GPT-class models' Spanish corrective feedback is (missed errors, false corrections, over-normalization toward peninsular or 'textbook' Spanish rather than Mexican colloquial norms) is unmeasured — the published LLM-WCF studies are almost all English or Chinese.
- Extensive reading/listening meta-analytic effects are confounded with time-on-task; whether ER beats an equal-time alternative (e.g., intensive reading + SRS) for this learner's goals is unresolved.
- The captioned-video literature measures mostly short-term comprehension/vocabulary; long-term effects of habitual caption use on unassisted listening (caption dependence) are under-researched.
- Lexical-coverage thresholds (95%/98%) derive mainly from English materials; Spanish frequency-coverage curves differ (richer morphology), so the graded-reader→news→fiction progression should be calibrated by the learner's actual comprehension, not word-list coverage alone.
