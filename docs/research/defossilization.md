# defossilization

## Summary
The learner's premise — a "rusty AP-4" with ingrained errors who wants to fix them — sits directly on top of the single most pessimistic claim in the literature (Higgs & Clifford's 1982 "terminal 2/2+") and the single most influential deflation of that claim (Long 2003). The honest synthesis is optimistic but heavily qualified. The strong form of fossilization — a permanent, biologically-fixed cognitive endpoint that makes certain errors literally unfixable — is NOT empirically established. Long (2003) shows that in 30+ years no study actually demonstrated fossilization; studies "assumed, not demonstrated" it, used inappropriate subjects, insufficient data, and type-level (not token-level) analyses. Because "permanence" is unfalsifiable within a lifetime, and because renewed development after multi-year plateaus is well-attested, Long argues the field should study STABILIZATION (a real, observable plateau) rather than fossilization. Higgs & Clifford's terminal-2 claim — that learners who built vocabulary/fluency ahead of grammar "peak out" at ILR 2+ and are "not remediable even in intensive training" — turns out to rest on clinical observation of government (CIA/FSI/DLI) proficiency screening plus a subjective survey of ~50 teachers rating which subskill "matters most" per level. It was never a controlled remediation study. Its mechanism (proactive interference) is real in principle, but the "learning-proof" conclusion is exactly the kind of impressionistic, unfalsifiable claim Long critiques. So: the learner should treat "terminal 2" as a risk profile and a warning about premature fluency-over-accuracy, not as a verdict.

On the positive side, the remediation evidence is genuinely encouraging. Corrective-feedback meta-analyses find medium-to-large, durable effects (Li 2010: d≈0.64, maintained over time; Lyster & Saito 2010: prompts d≈0.83, explicit correction d≈0.84, recasts d≈0.53 — explicit/prompting beats implicit recasting for stabilized forms). Skill-acquisition theory (DeKeyser & Suzuki) explains the mechanism and the cost: declarative knowledge proceduralizes fast (DeKeyser 1997: essentially complete after ~16 practice items), but automatization is a slow power-law grind with no true endpoint, and — critically — procedural skill is highly SPECIFIC (comprehension practice doesn't transfer to production, receptive to productive, or speaking to writing). This is the most important architectural fact for a voice-only plan: an hour of talking automatizes talking, not writing, and not the specific error unless that error is the target of practice. Skill-acquisition theory has no clean account of "overwriting" an automatized error; it mostly explains building new skills, and the old habit competes (proactive interference is the pessimists' point). The practical answer researchers converge on is not "practice more of the same" but focused, explicit attention (focus on form / consciousness-raising / prompted output) at the moment of error, repeated over time — which is precisely what an externally-logged, recurring error-treatment loop can deliver.

For sequencing the Spanish grammar work, the research gives a clear map of what is teachable/acquirable at B1+ versus what stays late and variable. Acquirable with focused work at this level: preterite/imperfect FORMS (forms precede accurate use), ser/estar (fully masterable at later stages though English speakers overuse ser and lag on estar), clitic placement and number, article/agreement basics. Late, variable, or interface-vulnerable even at advanced levels: the appropriate USE of imperfect in non-prototypical/backgrounding contexts; the subjunctive (acquired late, resists automatization, gated by complex-syntax development, low salience and low communicative value); the syntax-discourse interface generally (null/overt subjects, clitic-doubling pragmatics); por/para and other many-to-one L1 mappings. Two theoretical constraints matter for sequencing: (1) the Aspect/Lexical Aspect Hypothesis (Andersen & Shirai) predicts perfective spreads from telic verbs outward and imperfective from statives outward — but it is contested for instructed L2 Spanish (Salaberry's Default Past Tense Hypothesis found beginners use preterite as an all-purpose default regardless of lexical aspect; Domínguez et al. 2013 found dynamicity, not telicity, drove choices). Importantly, Montrul & Slabakova and others show advanced L2ers CAN attain complete underlying aspect representations — defossilization of aspect is possible. (2) Processability/Teachability Theory (Pienemann) says processing-based developmental stages can't be skipped by instruction, though instruction can accelerate movement into the next reachable stage — a real constraint on the subjunctive, since Collentine's "syntactic deficiency hypothesis" holds learners can't reliably select mood until they can reliably produce subordinate clauses (though Farley's processing-instruction studies claim to push past predicted readiness — contested).

Bottom line for the plan: (a) The learner is very unlikely to be a true "terminal" case; stabilized errors in a motivated adult are treatable, but the treatment must be explicit, targeted, spaced, and production-focused, not just more conversation. (b) Budget the 500 hours with the skill-specificity tax in mind: conversation automatizes speaking, so most defossilization value comes from making the SPOKEN error the explicit target repeatedly. (c) Sequence early/teachable morphology (pret/imp forms, ser/estar, clitics, agreement) ahead of late/interface features (subjunctive mood-selection, imperfect discourse use, por/para nuance, subject pragmatics), and treat the subjunctive as a long, syntax-gated project rather than a unit to "finish." (d) The error-log + recurring focus-on-form architecture the learner proposes is well-aligned with the corrective-feedback and focus-on-form evidence base, but note honestly that the specific packaging (self-directed AI voice tutor + externally engineered memory) has essentially no direct empirical validation — it is a reasonable extrapolation, not a proven protocol.

## Findings
### Selinker's original fossilization claim was strong and explicitly permanent
Selinker (1972) introduced fossilization as a putative cognitive MECHANISM in the 'latent psychological structure': 'Fossilizable linguistic phenomena are linguistic items, rules, and subsystems which speakers of a particular NL will tend to keep in their IL relative to a particular TL, no matter what the age of the learner or the amount of explanation and instruction he receives.' It was meant to explain 'backsliding' — the reappearance of errors thought to be eradicated — and characterized the L2 end-state as permanent retention of deviant rules. He anecdotally claimed ~95% of adult L2 learners fail to reach native competence. Crucially, permanence and imperviousness-to-instruction were built into the original definition, which is what makes it both influential and (per Long) untestable.
Sources: https://www.blackwellpublishing.com/content/bpl_images/content_store/WWW_Content/9780631217541/17Chap16.pdf; https://simple.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interlanguage_fossilization

### Long (2003) argues permanent fossilization is empirically unproven and probably the wrong construct — use 'stabilization' instead
Long's chapter 'Stabilization and Fossilization in Interlanguage Development' is the pivotal critique. Core arguments: (1) A claim that something in a person is 'permanent' is UNFALSIFIABLE within their lifetime, yet permanence is the ONLY property distinguishing fossilization from stabilization. (2) In 30+ years of research the empirical evidence for fossilization is 'vanishingly small' — studies suffered from four flaws: assuming rather than demonstrating fossilization; selecting inappropriate learners; basing findings on insufficient data; and using inadequate (type-level, not token-level) analyses. (3) U-shaped behavior and renewed development after plateaus 'lasting several years' are well-attested in normal acquisition, so a multi-year plateau does NOT prove permanence. (4) Selinker himself required 2–5 years of observation to substantiate a fossilization claim. Long concludes fossilization 'may occur, but until research shows convincingly that it does,' researchers should describe the well-attested phenomenon of stabilization. The strongest candidate case (Lardiere's 'Patty,' ~10 yrs immersion, past-tense marking stable at ~34% suppliance) actually shows intact underlying syntax despite low morphology — i.e., a production/mapping problem, not a frozen grammar.
Sources: https://www.blackwellpublishing.com/content/bpl_images/content_store/WWW_Content/9780631217541/17Chap16.pdf; https://benjamins.com/catalog/bpa.14.03pie

### The Higgs & Clifford (1982) 'terminal 2/2+' claim: what the actual evidence was
In 'The Push Toward Communication,' Higgs & Clifford described the 'terminal 2' — learners who enter government (CIA/FSI/DLI) training at ILR Level 2 and 'peak out' at 2+, never reaching Level 3, showing a 'classic profile' of HIGH vocabulary/fluency but LOW grammatical accuracy (fossilized incorrect patterns, not merely absent ones). They claimed such patterns are 'not remediable, even in intensive language training programs or additional in-country living experience' — 'learning proof.' The EVIDENCE was: (a) clinical/observational profiling of government screening cases using a 'Performance Profile' rating form ('experience has shown again and again,' 'our vast experience'); and (b) a SUBJECTIVE survey in which ~50 CIA Language School teachers made pairwise judgments of which subskill mattered most at each proficiency level (converted to percentages, Figure 4). There was NO controlled remediation study, no longitudinal data, and no experimental defossilization attempt. Their causal explanation was proactive interference (prior task A interfering with task B; interference grows with the strength/duration/frequency of A). They attributed most terminal cases to 'street learning' or to teachers who didn't correct errors. This is precisely the 'assumed, not demonstrated' evidence Long later critiqued — and it directly targets a profile like the learner's (AP-4 + years of uncorrected travel Spanish).
Sources: https://sb54e2396517e46e6.jimcontent.com/download/version/1715933471/module/5947544062/name/Higgs%20and%20Clifford%201982%20(PronSci),%20The%20Push%20Toward%20Communication.pdf; https://web.pdx.edu/~fischerw/~fischer/courses/advanced/methods_docs/pdf_doc/wbf_collection/0101_0150/0103_FLA85_Higgs_gr.PDF

### Later work did not confirm the strong 'terminal/unremediable' claim; the construct was softened to 'selective' and probabilistic
The terminal-2 hypothesis's subskill-weighting model (grammar becomes more important than vocabulary above Level 2) has some face validity and is echoed in proficiency pedagogy, but the strong 'learning-proof/not remediable' verdict has not survived. Han's 'Forty years later: Updating the Fossilization Hypothesis' (2013) recasts the phenomenon as the SELECTIVE Fossilization Hypothesis (Han 2009): not all structures fossilize, and susceptibility is a probabilistic function of L1 markedness and L2 input robustness for a specific feature — a far weaker claim than a fixed terminal ceiling for a whole learner. Fidler (2006) similarly 'reconceptualizes' fossilization. Net: modern SLA treats persistent errors as feature-specific, input- and L1-conditioned stabilizations that are hard but not categorically permanent — not as a global cognitive terminus.
Sources: https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/language-teaching/article/abs/forty-years-later-updating-the-fossilization-hypothesis/FC11A96BBCBF0790994DC1FE26915052; https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1191/0267658306sr273ra

### Corrective feedback CAN measurably remediate errors, with medium-to-large and durable effects
Two meta-analyses anchor the defossilization optimism. Li (2010, Language Learning) found an overall CF effect of d≈0.64 (medium) that was MAINTAINED (and in some contuments grew) on delayed post-tests. Lyster & Saito (2010, SSLA) found CF effects of d≈0.53 for recasts, d≈0.83 for prompts, and d≈0.84 for explicit correction — i.e., more explicit/output-pushing feedback (prompts, elicitation, metalinguistic correction) outperforms implicit recasts, and effects were durable and larger for classroom (vs lab) studies. Implication for stabilized errors: implicit recasting (the default of a friendly conversation partner, human or AI) is the WEAKEST tool; the learner needs explicit, prompting feedback that forces self-repair. Caveat: most CF studies target discrete, treatable features over short spans and use learners who had not yet stabilized the form; direct evidence on de-automatizing a LONG-stabilized adult error is thinner.
Sources: https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1467-9922.2010.00561.x; https://www.scribd.com/document/878074641/Lyster-ORALFEEDBACKCLASSROOM-2010

### Skill-acquisition theory: proceduralization is fast, automatization is a slow power-law grind, and skills are highly specific
DeKeyser & Suzuki (2025): moving from declarative rule-knowledge to procedural control ('proceduralization') can be essentially complete after just a few trials — DeKeyser (1997) found it complete after the first 16-item practice block. But 'automatization' (further reductions in reaction time and error rate) follows the POWER LAW of practice: a steep early drop then an ever-flattening curve that 'never reaches an absolute endpoint.' Most importantly, procedural knowledge is SKILL-SPECIFIC: practice in comprehension does not transfer well to production, nor receptive to productive, nor (by extension) speaking to writing. Practice must be 'deliberate' — systematic, targeted activity aimed at improving the specific skill, tightly coupled with feedback. This is the single most consequential fact for a voice-only plan: conversation automatizes the act of conversing; it does not automatically fix a specific error or build writing/reading skill unless those are separately, deliberately targeted.
Sources: https://yuichisuzuki.net/wp-content/uploads/2025/07/PreprintDeKeyser-R.-M.-Suzuki-Y.-2025.-Skill-acquisition-theory.-In-B.-VanPatten-G.-D.-Keating-S.-Wulff-Eds.-Theories-in-second-language-acquisition-An-introduction-4th-ed.-pp.-157-182-.pdf; https://www.researchgate.net/publication/277701391_Automatization_Skill_Acquisition_and_Practice_in_Second_Language_Acquisition

### The 'unlearning' problem: proactive interference is real, but skill theory has no clean 'overwrite' mechanism — you build a competing correct routine, you don't erase the old one
Higgs & Clifford's pessimism rests on proactive interference — a genuine phenomenon where strongly practiced prior learning (an automatized error) competes with new learning, more so when old and new are similar. Skill-acquisition theory largely models BUILDING skills, not deleting them; there is no established parameter for 'how many reps to overwrite an automatized error.' In practice, remediation means proceduralizing and then slowly automatizing a NEW correct routine that must out-compete the old one under real-time pressure — which is why stabilized errors resurface under cognitive load ('backsliding') even after the learner 'knows' the rule. This predicts that (a) monitored/planned production shows the correct form long before spontaneous speech does, and (b) durable change requires many spaced retrievals of the correct form specifically in production, not one-time explanation. There is no credible published number for 'X hours overwrites an error'; be skeptical of any such claim.
Sources: https://sb54e2396517e46e6.jimcontent.com/download/version/1715933471/module/5947544062/name/Higgs%20and%20Clifford%201982%20(PronSci),%20The%20Push%20Toward%20Communication.pdf; https://yuichisuzuki.net/wp-content/uploads/2025/07/PreprintDeKeyser-R.-M.-Suzuki-Y.-2025.-Skill-acquisition-theory.-In-B.-VanPatten-G.-D.-Keating-S.-Wulff-Eds.-Theories-in-second-language-acquisition-An-introduction-4th-ed.-pp.-157-182-.pdf

### Direct defossilization interventions exist and show gains — but the literature is small and mostly on discrete features
Beyond the CF meta-analyses, targeted defossilization studies report success: consciousness-raising / focused form-focused remedial instruction with explicit negative feedback improved acquisition of target features (e.g., Chan & Li 2002); corpus-driven input- and output-based instruction 'defossilized' fossilized pragmatic routines in advanced learners. But some stabilized categories resist even sustained treatment — one study found errors in pronoun, word order, passive, and possessive persisted despite both implicit and explicit CF. So the honest picture is: many stabilized errors move with intensive explicit treatment; a subset (often L1-transfer-driven, interface-level) is stubborn. Intensity that 'moved' errors in studies is typically multiple focused sessions per feature over weeks, not a single correction — but no consensus dosage exists.
Sources: https://www.academia.edu/39822496/Defossilization_of_Fossilized_Pragmatic_Routines_Corpus_Driven_Input_Based_and_Output_Based_Instruction; https://www.researchgate.net/publication/312328329_Form-focused_instruction_Effectiveness_of_consciousness-raising_tasks_and_direct_grammar_instruction_in_EFL_learning

### Preterite/imperfect: forms come before accurate use; the Lexical Aspect Hypothesis is influential but contested for instructed L2 Spanish
Rothman, González Alonso & Miller (2018) summarize a robust three-stage progression: (1) reliance on lexico-pragmatics/adverbials with no past morphology; (2) adverbials mark time but verb morphology still doesn't; (3) morphology emerges — PERFECTIVE (preterite) first, appearing initially on achievements/accomplishments (telic) then spreading to activities/states; IMPERFECTIVE later, appearing first on statives then spreading. 'Forms precede meanings' — learners produce both tenses before using them contextually correctly, and the imperfect (more semantically varied, more non-prototypical/backgrounding uses) is harder and later. The Lexical Aspect Hypothesis (Andersen & Shirai 1994; prototype/Congruence/Relevance principles) predicts these spreads. BUT it is contested for instructed learners: Salaberry's Default Past Tense Hypothesis found beginning classroom L2-Spanish learners use the PRETERITE as an all-purpose default regardless of lexical aspect (conflicting with LAH), and Domínguez et al. (2013) found DYNAMICITY, not telicity, drove learner choices, with no clean spread across lexical classes.
Sources: https://centaur.reading.ac.uk/73203/1/The%20Acquisition%20of%20L2%20Spanish%20Morphosyntax.pdf; https://muse.jhu.edu/article/531380; https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/abs/aspect-hypothesis-and-the-acquisition-of-l2-past-morphology-in-the-last-20-years/353744B9E21B7B88989CD96A45E3B87F

### Good news for a false-intermediate: advanced L2ers CAN attain full underlying aspect representations, and instruction/sequencing can help
Formal-linguistic studies (Montrul & Slabakova; Slabakova & Montrul) show that at advanced proficiency L2 learners of Spanish can have COMPLETE representations of grammatical aspect — i.e., preterite/imperfect competence is attainable, not permanently fossilized. Sequencing may matter: a 2025 program-wide intervention (Sánchez-Gutiérrez et al., Foreign Language Annals, N=697) reversed the usual order and taught the IMPERFECT before the preterite, on the rationale that teaching preterite first contributes to lasting difficulty with the imperfect; obligatory tense-aspect context and task modality were significant predictors of accuracy. Practical read: the imperfect (especially its backgrounding/habitual and non-prototypical uses) is the harder, later half and deserves disproportionate, explicit, front-loaded attention rather than being treated as the 'easy' regular conjugation.
Sources: https://centaur.reading.ac.uk/73203/1/The%20Acquisition%20of%20L2%20Spanish%20Morphosyntax.pdf; https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/flan.70012

### Subjunctive: acquired late, resists automatization, gated by complex-syntax development, and low-salience/low-communicative-value
Collentine (2010) synthesizes: the subjunctive is only ~7.2% of native verb forms (low frequency in input), is 'poorly developed even after numerous formal opportunities,' and 'resists automatization.' His Syntactic Deficiency Hypothesis: because the subjunctive lives in subordinate clauses, learners can't reliably select mood until they can reliably PRODUCE subordinate clauses (a complex-syntax prerequisite; cf. Pienemann/processability). By the end of a typical 4-semester sequence learners have 'learned' but not 'acquired' it — they can't deploy it in meaningful speech. UG/interface accounts (Montrul; Sorace) treat the syntax–discourse-pragmatics interface as the most 'vulnerable' area for adults, explaining heavy L1 transfer and persistent non-native use; even heritage speakers lose subjunctive before pret/imperfect. Salience is low (most subjunctive forms differ from indicative only by a thematic vowel), so implicit exposure alone 'holds little promise.'
Sources: https://faculty.weber.edu/tmathews/sli/readings/collentine%202010.pdf; https://www2.nau.edu/~jgc/research/subjstate/index.htm

### Subjunctive is nonetheless teachable — processing instruction and explicit strategy help, and may push past 'readiness' (contested)
Collentine (2010): input-oriented PROCESSING INSTRUCTION (VanPatten/Lee) is the best-supported method — it explicitly tells learners they tend to ignore verbal mood and that the subjunctive carries pragmatic meaning, outperforming plain structured input (Farley 2004a; Fernández 2008). Farley (2004b) showed PI can 'nullify syntactic deficiencies' by decomposing the task (process the main clause, then focus on the subordinate mood), producing sustained mood-selection gains. Farley & McCollam and McCollam-Weibe (within a processability framework) even report PI achieving results BEYOND learners' predicted readiness — challenging Collentine's syntactic-deficiency gate (this is contested and not settled). Gudmestad found the subjunctive is first integrated as 'just another conjugation' (irregular forms + volition verbs), then accumulates semantic categories with development; task type massively affects production (free monologue << controlled subordinate-clause production). Study abroad alone yields only ~40–50% mood-selection accuracy after 4 months; explicit instruction plus complex-syntax ability matters more than immersion.
Sources: https://faculty.weber.edu/tmathews/sli/readings/collentine%202010.pdf; https://jan.ucc.nau.edu/jgc/research/pi/

### Ser/estar: acquired in stages, English speakers overuse ser and lag on estar — but the contrast IS masterable at later stages
VanPatten (1985, oral interviews over a year) established that adult L2ers acquire ser before estar and pass through developmental stages; early English-speaking learners univocally map 'to be' → ser (full transfer) and OVERUSE ser in locatives (VanPatten's stage 2), acquiring estar later. Rothman et al. (2018): most work shows English speakers (whose L1 lacks the two-copula distinction) have difficulty converging on the distributional patterns throughout development, BUT 'at later stages this contrast can be fully mastered.' Complications: ser/estar is dialectally variable and synchronically changing (a 'moving target'), and estar is spreading in some varieties — so 'native-like' is itself a range. Processing Instruction has also been shown effective for ser/estar. Verdict: a treatable, sequence-able feature where the specific fix is suppressing ser-overuse and building estar in adjectival/locative/progressive contexts.
Sources: https://centaur.reading.ac.uk/73203/1/The%20Acquisition%20of%20L2%20Spanish%20Morphosyntax.pdf; https://www.scribd.com/document/732341389/VanPatten-B-1985-The-Acquisition-of-ser-and-estar-by-Adult-Learners-of-Spanish

### Clitic pronouns and object marking: placement/number acquired earlier, gender agreement and interpretation later; the First-Noun Principle drives errors
Rothman et al. (2018): English speakers' L1 lacks object clitics as a category entirely, yet clitics ARE acquirable — correct placement (pre-finite-verb / attached to nonfinite forms), case, and even sociolinguistic variation (leísmo) can be attained, despite developmental errors in surface case (dative/accusative confusion) and word order. Acquisition order (multiple studies): correct placement + number agreement first, then appropriate interpretation, then gender agreement last; singular before plural, masculine before feminine; beginners OMIT clitics, over-using full NPs. VanPatten's First-Noun Principle explains a signature processing error: learners parse the first noun/pronoun as the subject/agent, so preverbal object clitics (e.g., 'Lo vio María') get misread as agents — a comprehension error that Processing Instruction specifically targets. Object-drop restrictions and clitic-doubling pragmatics (syntax-discourse interface) are later and more variable.
Sources: https://centaur.reading.ac.uk/73203/1/The%20Acquisition%20of%20L2%20Spanish%20Morphosyntax.pdf; https://benjamins.com/catalog/lia.00009.pea

### por/para and other many-to-one L1 mappings are persistently difficult and late; agreement is late despite early teaching
por/para is a classic difficulty: English 'for' maps onto two Spanish prepositions with functional/aspectual distinctions, and per the Hierarchy of Difficulty, one-L1-form-to-two-L2-forms splits are among the hardest to acquire; they remain error-prone even with explicit instruction. Similarly, adjective/noun gender-number AGREEMENT is 'late acquired despite being taught early,' especially for L1s without grammatical gender — agreement is a persistent variable-error zone (a likely stabilized-error candidate for this learner). The syntax-DISCOURSE interface features — null vs overt subject pragmatics (topic shift/contrastive focus) — are acquired much later than the core syntax 'if at all to native-likeness' (Lozano; Montrul & Rodríguez Louro; Rothman). These are the features to treat as long-horizon, accept-variability targets rather than 'fixable this year.'
Sources: https://centaur.reading.ac.uk/73203/1/The%20Acquisition%20of%20L2%20Spanish%20Morphosyntax.pdf; https://euroslajournal.org/articles/48/files/submission/proof/48-1-617-1-10-20180831.pdf

### Processability/Teachability Theory: instruction can't skip processing stages, but can accelerate movement into the next reachable stage
Pienemann's Processability Theory posits six processing-based stages (words → categorical morphology → phrasal → inter-phrasal/sentential → subordinate-clause procedures); at any stage the learner can only produce forms the current processor can handle, and these constraints are claimed to be immune to context/instruction. The Teachability Hypothesis follows: formal instruction facilitates a structure only if the learner's interlanguage is at the immediately preceding stage; stages cannot be skipped, but instruction can ACCELERATE progress into the next reachable stage (and teaching a higher stage can pull the learner through intervening ones). Practical constraint for this plan: readiness gates what grammar work can pay off NOW — e.g., mood-selection across clause boundaries and inter-phrasal agreement presuppose the relevant processing procedures. This is a real (if debated) reason to sequence by processing prerequisites, not just by textbook order — and a reason the subjunctive is a late, syntax-gated project. Note the tension with Farley's PI findings claiming gains beyond predicted readiness; the field has not fully reconciled these.
Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Teachability_Hypothesis; https://scispace.com/pdf/pienemann-s-teachability-hypothesis-and-processability-7g4739n29p.pdf

### Error logs + recurring focus-on-form: aligned with the evidence base, but the specific self-directed/AI packaging is unvalidated extrapolation
The proposed architecture (persistent error log driving recurring focus-on-form) is CONSISTENT with strong evidence: focus-on-form (drawing attention to form within meaningful communication) and explicit/prompted corrective feedback have durable medium-large effects (Li 2010; Lyster & Saito 2010), and error-log studies in L2 writing find that logging + explaining one's corrections predicts improved accuracy in new writing. But candor is warranted: (1) that evidence is mostly from teacher-led classrooms and writing, not from a self-directed learner using a real-time AI voice partner with externally engineered memory — that exact protocol has ~no direct empirical validation; (2) recasts (the AI's likely default gentle correction) are the weakest CF type, so the system must be engineered to PROMPT self-repair, not just model the correct form; (3) skill-specificity means logging a spoken error and drilling it in writing won't fully transfer to speech — the treatment must recur in spoken production. So: the pattern is evidence-aligned scaffolding, not folklore, but it is an informed design bet, not a proven method.
Sources: https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0346251X24001118; https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1467-9922.2010.00561.x

## Design implications
- Treat 'terminal 2' as a risk model, not a diagnosis. The learner is a motivated adult with explicit metalinguistic knowledge (AP-4) — the opposite of the uncorrected 'street learner' Higgs & Clifford profiled. Stabilized errors here are treatable; the plan's job is to prevent the terminal trajectory (fluency racing ahead of accuracy), which means building explicit accuracy monitoring in from day one rather than 'communicating first, fixing later.'
- Engineer the AI to PROMPT, not recast. The default behavior of a friendly voice model is implicit recasting — the weakest corrective-feedback type (d≈0.53 vs ~0.83–0.84 for prompts/explicit correction). Instruct the model (via system prompt/curriculum files) to withhold the answer and elicit self-repair on logged target errors ('You just said X — try that verb again'), then confirm. Reserve implicit recasts for non-target errors to protect fluency.
- Budget hours against skill-specificity. Conversation automatizes speaking, and procedural skill barely transfers (comprehension↛production, speaking↛writing). If speaking accuracy is the goal, the bulk of the 500 hours should be spoken PRODUCTION with the target error as the explicit object; add separate deliberate blocks for any writing/reading/listening goals rather than assuming conversation covers them.
- Make defossilization spaced and retrieval-based, not one-shot. Because you build a competing correct routine rather than erase the old one, each stabilized error needs many spaced production retrievals over weeks, with difficulty rising toward spontaneous (unmonitored) use. The external memory should schedule each active error for recurring re-elicitation and track spontaneous-vs-prompted accuracy separately (backsliding under load is expected and is the real test of durability).
- Sequence by teachability/processing prerequisites and by early-vs-late acquisition. Front-load features that are acquirable and treatable now: preterite/imperfect FORMS, ser/estar (kill ser-overuse; build estar), clitic placement+number, gender-number agreement, high-frequency por/para contrasts. Defer or run as long-horizon projects the late/interface features: imperfect discourse/backgrounding use, subjunctive mood-selection, subject null/overt pragmatics, fine por/para nuance.
- Treat the subjunctive as a syntax-gated, multi-month strand, not a unit. Ensure the learner can reliably produce subordinate clauses first (Collentine's prerequisite). Use processing-instruction-style tasks that flag the low salience ('you tend to ignore the mood ending') and decompose the task (main clause → then subordinate mood). Expect slow, task-dependent gains and don't treat completion of a 'subjunctive unit' as acquisition.
- Give the imperfect disproportionate, front-loaded attention. Forms precede correct use, and the imperfect (more semantically varied, more non-prototypical/backgrounding uses) is the harder half; a 2025 intervention even reversed order to teach imperfect first. Design pret/imp practice around aspectual CONTRAST in narrative (foreground/background) rather than isolated conjugation drills, and use verbs across all lexical-aspect classes to break the default-preterite habit.
- Log errors with feature tags tied to acquisition status, and set realistic targets per tag. Mark each logged error as 'early/treatable' (drive to spontaneous mastery), 'late/variable' (aim for monitored accuracy + gradual improvement), or 'interface/likely-persistent' (por/para nuance, subject pragmatics — accept variability). This prevents wasting intensive treatment on features research says stay variable even at advanced levels.
- Instrument for measurement, not vibes. There is no credible 'X hours overwrites an error' number; treat dosage as empirical. Track per-error accuracy over time (prompted and spontaneous) and reallocate intensity to errors that are actually moving, retiring those that plateau despite sustained explicit treatment (a signal to accept variability, not add reps).

## Open questions
- Dosage is unknown: no study establishes how many spaced, focused production retrievals are needed to de-automatize a long-stabilized adult error. The plan should treat this as an experiment to measure per-feature, not a parameter to look up.
- External validity gap: virtually all the supporting evidence (CF meta-analyses, focus-on-form, error logs) comes from teacher-led classrooms and often writing; none validates a self-directed learner using a real-time AI voice partner with engineered cross-session memory. The architecture is evidence-aligned but empirically unproven as a package.
- Unresolved theoretical tension: Pienemann's teachability (readiness gates instruction; can't skip stages) vs Farley's processing-instruction results claiming subjunctive gains beyond predicted readiness. It is unclear how hard the readiness constraint really binds for a metalinguistically sophisticated adult, which affects how aggressively the subjunctive can be pushed.
- The Lexical Aspect Hypothesis is contested for INSTRUCTED L2 Spanish (Salaberry's default-preterite finding; Domínguez et al.'s dynamicity-not-telicity result). It is unclear whether pret/imp practice should be organized around lexical-aspect prototypes at all, or around foreground/background discourse function and dynamicity instead.
- Can an AI voice model reliably detect the learner's specific errors in real time (especially subtle ones: aspect misuse, mood, clitic gender, agreement) well enough to prompt targeted self-repair? Feedback accuracy/consistency of GPT-Live-1 on Spanish morphosyntax is untested and is a single point of failure for the whole error-treatment loop.
- How to reliably transfer monitored/planned accuracy (which appears early) into spontaneous, cognitively-loaded speech (which lags and backslides)? The mechanism for closing that gap — beyond 'more spaced production under increasing load' — is not well specified in the literature for adult remediation.
- Which of THIS learner's specific stabilized errors are L1-transfer/interface-driven (more resistant) vs simple under-practiced forms (more treatable)? An early diagnostic is needed because the two categories warrant different intensity and different success criteria, and the research only predicts categories, not individuals.
