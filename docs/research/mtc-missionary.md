# mtc-missionary

## Summary
The Provo MTC is best understood as a short, brutally intensive on-ramp rather than a fluency machine. Since 2013, Spanish (Romance-language) missionaries spend 6 weeks there (previously 8-9), living a 6:30am-10:30pm schedule with roughly 10-12 hours a day of combined language and religious training, six days a week — on the order of 350-450 total contact hours, of which perhaps half is explicitly language-focused. The pedagogy is aggressively task-based: from the first days missionaries pray, bear testimony, and teach role-played "investigators" in the target language, long before they have general vocabulary ("learn to pray and preach in the new language before they learn common terms" — Salt Lake Tribune). The binding constraint is the SYL ("Speak Your Language") commitment — try Spanish first, ask permission before using English, keep a notebook of every word you lacked, say new things every day — plus a daily plan-study-teach cycle, companion accountability, and one hour a day in the TALL computer lab (now the public TALL Embark app) doing spaced vocabulary/phrase drills, grammar exercises, and record-and-compare pronunciation against native models. Preach My Gospel Chapter 7 is the official method statement: weekly/daily goals, a personal study plan, flashcards, active listening, seeking feedback, and a weekly evaluation — strikingly close to mainstream self-regulated-learning and task-based language teaching, with essentially no gamification.

The honest outcome story splits cleanly in two. Missionaries leave the MTC functional only within their narrow domain — able to teach prepared lessons, pray, and survive scripted interactions; even sympathetic accounts concede "fluency is still months away." There is no published ACTFL/OPI data at MTC exit (the MTC uses internal instruments — Bateman's Missionary Language Performance Test, and later a Spanish elicited-imitation test). The famous result comes from the following ~22 months: Dewey & Clifford (2012), testing recently returned missionaries across seven languages with official OPIs, found most at ACTFL Advanced with ~80% at Advanced-Mid/Advanced-High — roughly CEFR B2, about one full level above graduating US language majors. But Ray Clifford himself stresses that only ~6% reach Superior and that returned missionaries show "consistent patterns of grammatical and vocabulary deficiencies"; he attributes the outcome to 4,000-6,000 hours of use over two years. A 2025 BYU study of 2,475 students found the strongest non-linguistic predictors of Spanish oral proficiency were mission experience and the proportion of native-speaker companions — i.e., interactive time with natives, not the 6-week MTC, does most of the work. What the MTC demonstrably contributes is a running start, trained study habits, an accountability architecture, and (per NPR and researchers) extremely high purpose-driven motivation.

For a solo learner with an AI voice partner, the transferable mechanisms are the SYL protocol (target-language-only with a formal English escape hatch and a "words I lacked" log), organizing every session around a communicative task with a deliverable (especially teach-to-learn: preparing and delivering short "lessons" to an AI playing a questioning listener — the TRC role-play analog), daily verbal goal-setting and end-of-session self-evaluation, a weekly written review of the study plan, memorized functional scripts that get progressively personalized (with PMG's own warning not to hide behind them), a separate daily SRS/pronunciation drill block (the TALL analog), and teaching yourself the rating criteria — a BYU dissertation (Radford 2014) found that simply teaching MTC learners the speaking-performance rubric increased proficiency. What does not transfer: the 24/7 cohort immersion, the live native companion (the single strongest predictor in the data), the social stakes of teaching real strangers, and the religious motivation/identity engine that observers consider central. The research is essentially all BYU-affiliated and observational — there are no controlled comparisons of MTC methods against alternatives — so treat the mechanisms as plausible best practices validated mostly by convergence with mainstream SLA findings, not as proven causes of the missionary outcome.

## Findings
### Since the 2013 restructuring, Spanish missionaries spend 6 weeks at the MTC (down from 8-9), hard languages (Mandarin, Russian, Finnish, etc.) get 9 weeks (down from 12), and English-speaking missionaries 2-3 weeks.
The Salt Lake Tribune's inside account states Romance languages (Spanish, French, Italian) require 6 weeks and that 31 of 55 languages taught require 9 weeks. A 2011 Deseret News feature (pre-reduction) documented the older 3/8/12-week tiers, and multiple sources tie the ~one-third reduction to the 2012-13 missionary-age change. Some North American Spanish missionaries instead train at the Mexico City MTC on a similar 6-week track. Durations have shifted repeatedly (including a home-MTC hybrid after 2020), so any single number should be treated as era-specific.
Sources: https://archive.sltrib.com/article.php?id=56556202&itype=CMSID; https://www.deseret.com/2011/3/21/20385134/mormon-church-s-provo-mtc-exclusive-look-at-the-largest-missionary-training-facility-in-the-world/; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Missionary_Training_Center; https://sojo.net/articles/inside-mormons-elite-missionary-training-center

### The MTC day runs 6:30am to 10:30pm with roughly 10-12 hours of combined language and religious training daily, six days a week — approximately 350-450 total contact hours for a 6-week Spanish missionary, of which explicit language work is only part.
The Tribune and Deseret News describe ~10-12 hours of daily instruction/study, one hour of exercise, Sunday services, and a weekly preparation day. Unofficial sources estimate ~6 hours/day of language-specific training; the rest is doctrine, increasingly conducted in the target language. So even the MTC's 'miracle' phase involves a hardware budget comparable to the learner's entire planned 500 hours — compressed into 6 weeks of full immersion.
Sources: https://archive.sltrib.com/article.php?id=56556202&itype=CMSID; https://www.deseret.com/2011/3/21/20385134/mormon-church-s-provo-mtc-exclusive-look-at-the-largest-missionary-training-facility-in-the-world/; https://www.ldsliving.com/whats-life-like-at-the-mtc/s/78125

### SYL ('Speak Your Language') is a formalized commitment with five specific operational rules, not just a slogan.
The official MTC manual page specifies: (1) use everything you already know; (2) always attempt the mission language first, even unsure; (3) if you truly can't say it, formally ask permission to say it in English; (4) keep a notebook of words and phrases you lacked and learn them; (5) deliberately say new things each day. A missionary testimonial on the page describes companionships systematically learning ~20 new words/day this way. The 'ask permission' rule is the enforcement mechanism that makes code-switching costly instead of free.
Sources: https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/manual/a-missionarys-mtc-experience-language-2015/language/speak-your-language?lang=eng

### The curriculum is task-based and purpose-driven: language is learned through the job of teaching missionary lessons, with role-played 'investigators' from the first week, not through a grammar sequence.
NPR's 2014 feature describes context-based learning, role-play, and quotes Betty Lou Leaver (Defense Language Institute) framing it as task-based instruction ('a task is something you might actually do in your life'). The Deseret News documents the Training Resource Center: 30 rooms where missionaries teach volunteer role-players and get video review, plus progressive Teaching Evaluation sessions. The Tribune notes missionaries 'learn to pray and preach in the new language before they learn common terms.' An MTC teacher: 'it's not just Spanish class, but it's for a purpose.'
Sources: https://www.npr.org/2014/06/07/319805068/lessons-from-the-language-boot-camp-for-mormon-missionaries; https://www.deseret.com/2011/3/21/20385134/mormon-church-s-provo-mtc-exclusive-look-at-the-largest-missionary-training-facility-in-the-world/; https://archive.sltrib.com/article.php?id=56556202&itype=CMSID; https://news-au.churchofjesuschrist.org/article/preparation-with-purpose--life-in-a-mormon-missionary-training-center

### The Church deliberately moved away from verbatim memorized scripts: before 2004 missionaries memorized six discussions word-for-word in the mission language; Preach My Gospel (2004) replaced this with teaching 'in your own words,' and current guidance explicitly warns against lengthy memorized scripts.
A BYU Religious Studies Center history records that by 1999, 52 of 54 surveyed US missions required word-for-word memorization with strict certification — and that this produced rote, unengaged teaching. PMG Chapter 7 still recommends memorizing key phrases and scriptures but states: 'you should not use lengthy memorized or prepared scripts as a way to avoid mistakes.' So the MTC's own 60-year experiment concluded that scripts are scaffolding to be personalized and outgrown, not the method itself.
Sources: https://rsc-dev.byu.edu/archived/volume-14-number-1-2013/history-preach-my-gospel; https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/manual/preach-my-gospel-a-guide-to-missionary-service/how-can-i-better-learn-my-mission-language?lang=eng

### Preach My Gospel Chapter 7 is the official method document: personal responsibility, weekly/daily goals, an 8-component written study plan, a vocabulary notebook and flashcards, active listening drills, feedback-seeking, and a weekly evaluation with companion and leaders.
Its four principles are: take responsibility; make study meaningful (tie it to real upcoming communication tasks); seek to communicate ('There is no substitute for talking with native speakers'); learn concepts thoroughly (regular review in new situations). The study-plan components include goal-setting keyed to scheduled teaching events, vocabulary mined from upcoming lessons, grammar studied via patterns found in the texts being used, and weekly plan evaluation. This is mainstream self-regulated learning plus task-based teaching, codified for 19-year-olds.
Sources: https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/manual/preach-my-gospel-a-guide-to-missionary-service/how-can-i-better-learn-my-mission-language?lang=eng

### TALL (Technology Assisted Language Learning) was the MTC's in-house CALL system — one hour of lab time five days a week: vocabulary/phrase flashcards, record-and-compare pronunciation against native models, grammar exercises, and simulated listening interactions with native speakers, with systematic review 'the software never forgets.'
The official MTC manual describes daily logins, one-hour lab sessions 5x/week (a teacher joins one session), and claims TALL improves pronunciation 'better than any other activity at the MTC' (an internal, unpublished claim). BYU research on TALL (Betteridge 2011, MA thesis, n=86 missionaries) found the strongest predictors of success with the software were learner proactivity, goal-orientation, and heavy use of interactive features (glossary, listen-to-your-voice) — and that learners wanted more training in how to use the tool. A 2000 Deseret News piece reports slower learners found the system boring and wanted more grammar practice.
Sources: https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/manual/a-missionarys-mtc-experience-language-2015/language/tall-information?lang=eng; https://scholarsarchive.byu.edu/etd/2677; https://www.deseret.com/2000/4/7/19556704/mtc-language-program-evolving-to-better-meet-missionary-needs/

### TALL's successor, 'TALL Embark,' was redesigned for usability and released publicly in 2020 — any learner can use it (70+ languages, 2,500+ words, 500+ phrases, listening/reading/speaking/writing practice), though its vocabulary is heavily gospel-domain.
The app (iOS/Android) descends directly from the MTC lab software and is free to the public. For a secular learner its main interest is as a model — bite-size functional phrases with native audio and record-compare speaking — rather than as a primary tool, since content is organized around missionary tasks.
Sources: https://www.thisweekinmormons.com/2020/09/embark-missionary-language-learning-software-now-available-to-all-members/; https://apps.apple.com/us/app/tall-embark/id6502469985; https://play.google.com/store/apps/details?id=org.churchofjesuschrist.mw.embark&hl=en_US

### A BYU PhD dissertation found that explicitly teaching MTC missionaries the language-performance rating criteria — how their speaking would be judged — increased speaking proficiency versus controls.
Radford (2014, BYU PhD) used a 2x2 design with computer-based practice and instruction in performance criteria for Spanish learners at the MTC; 'teaching of language performance criteria increases speaking proficiency,' and trainees reported better self-evaluation and goal-setting. Separately, Thompson (2013, BYU) built a computerized Spanish elicited-imitation test so the MTC could cheaply measure oral proficiency at entry and exit — evidence the MTC treats cheap, frequent proficiency measurement as an operational necessity.
Sources: https://scholarsarchive.byu.edu/etd/3978; https://scholarsarchive.byu.edu/etd/3602

### The MTC experience formally continues in the field: 'The First 12 Weeks' program adds a trainer-led extra ~60 minutes of daily structured study for new missionaries, and all language-learning missionaries do 30-60 minutes of daily language study for their entire mission.
Introduced alongside the 2013 MTC shortening, the program pairs each new missionary with a trainer, covers eight Preach My Gospel fundamentals, includes 44 self-assessment questions across the 12 weeks, and was piloted in six missions with reported improvements in how quickly new missionaries became effective (Church-reported, no independent data). The standard missionary daily schedule includes 60 minutes personal study, 30-60 minutes companion study, and 30-60 minutes language study — meaning deliberate study never stops even during two years of immersion.
Sources: https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/church/news/new-missionary-in-field-training-extends-mtc-experience?_r=1&lang=eng; http://themissionpresidentswife.blogspot.com/2016/01/the-first-twelve-weeks-of-mission.html; https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/manual/missionary-standards-for-disciples-of-jesus-christ/02-missionary-organization-and-activities?lang=eng

### Missionaries leave the MTC functional only in their narrow task domain — there is no published ACTFL/OPI data at MTC exit, and even friendly journalism concedes 'fluency is still months away.'
The Tribune states plainly that fluency comes months later, in-country. The MTC's own exit measurement uses internal instruments: Bateman's (1995) Missionary Language Performance Test — a task-based test validated against the ACTFL OPI, showing measurable differences between first-week and final-week missionaries but with no published level distributions — and later the Thompson elicited-imitation test. Anecdotal accounts (widely echoed in missionary memoirs and forums) describe arriving in the field unable to understand natural native speech. The famous NPR claim that '5 weeks approximated 3 years of university study' is a student self-report, not a measurement.
Sources: https://archive.sltrib.com/article.php?id=56556202&itype=CMSID; https://scholarsarchive.byu.edu/etd/4510/; https://www.npr.org/2014/06/07/319805068/lessons-from-the-language-boot-camp-for-mormon-missionaries

### After the full ~2-year mission, returned missionaries test mostly at ACTFL Advanced (roughly CEFR B2): Dewey & Clifford's OPI study across seven languages found ~80% at Advanced-Mid/Advanced-High — about one level above graduating US language majors — but only ~6% reach Superior, with 'consistent patterns of grammatical and vocabulary deficiencies.'
Dewey & Clifford (2012), in Hansen (ed.), Second Language Acquisition Abroad: The LDS Missionary Experience (Benjamins, Studies in Bilingualism 45), administered standardized OPIs to recently returned missionaries (16-22 months abroad, seven languages). Ray Clifford's BYU devotional cites the comparison of 391 returned missionaries vs 501 language majors at five large universities: RMs were more proficient speakers, but only 6% reached Superior (the level needed for professional careers), and most had persistent grammar/vocabulary deficits limiting complex discussion. Jonathan Green (a linguist) independently characterizes fresh RMs as ~B2 speaking/listening with weaker reading, much weaker writing, and grammar gaps.
Sources: https://benjamins.com/catalog/sibil.45.03dew; https://speeches.byu.edu/talks/ray-clifford/language-learning-truly-educational-experience/; https://archive.timesandseasons.org/2012/12/missions-and-language-learning/index.html; https://benjamins.com/catalog/sibil.45

### The evidence attributes most of the famed outcome to field immersion volume and native interaction, not the MTC itself: Clifford estimates missionaries accumulate 4,000-6,000 hours of language use over two years, and the strongest measured predictors of oral proficiency are mission experience and the proportion of native-speaker companions.
Clifford's devotional explicitly frames the mission as 40-60 hours/week for two years ≈ 4,000-6,000 hours — half of Ericsson's 10,000-hour expertise benchmark. Ames (2025, BYU, n=2,475 undergraduates with OPIc scores) found mission language experience and native-companion ratio were the strongest non-linguistic predictors, while formal academic study showed limited influence. Larson-Hall & Dewey (2012) similarly examined input, aptitude, and motivation among 44 missionaries learning Japanese. Implication: the MTC provides a launch and a study system; the proficiency is bought with thousands of hours of purposeful interactive use.
Sources: https://speeches.byu.edu/talks/ray-clifford/language-learning-truly-educational-experience/; https://scholarsarchive.byu.edu/etd/11060; https://chooser.crossref.org/?doi=10.1075/sibil.45.04lar

### Motivation, purpose, and identity are treated by observers and researchers as load-bearing components of the system, and they are the hardest to replicate.
NPR quotes a missionary saying the faith component 'is really the only way you can... stand it here'; the doctrine of the 'gift of tongues' frames diligent speaking practice as qualifying for divine help, converting drudgery into devotion. Linguist Daniel Everett (quoted in the Subtitle podcast piece) cautions that missionary commitment stems from faith rather than replicable linguistic method. The MTC also benefits from selection: 18-24-year-olds, full-time, cohort-immersed, with social accountability at every level.
Sources: https://www.npr.org/2014/06/07/319805068/lessons-from-the-language-boot-camp-for-mormon-missionaries; https://subtitlepod-62956.medium.com/why-mormons-are-so-good-at-languages-2710a7770a91

### Language distance moderates everything: the missionary literature finds European languages are learned faster and retained better than Asian languages, so Spanish results are the system's best case.
Hansen's overview of the research program (studies in Brazil, Germany, Japan, Korea, Mexico, Spain, Taiwan) reports European languages are acquired faster by English speakers and retained better post-mission. The returned-missionary population is also one of the three most-studied groups in the L2 attrition literature (Bardovi-Harlig & Stringer 2010) — relevant to a 'rusty' learner: Hansen et al. (2002) found large 'savings' effects in relearning forgotten vocabulary even 1-45 years after the mission, supporting the premise that a false-intermediate relearner progresses much faster than a true beginner.
Sources: https://artshumanitieshawaii.org/assets/hansen,-lynne---the-lds-missionary-experience.pdf; https://benjamins.com/catalog/sibil.45

### The companion structure is a designed accountability and feedback machine: 24/7 pairing, 30-60 min daily companion study, senior companions deliberately assigning teaching responsibility to juniors, and feedback norms ('simple and practical feedback with kindness').
PMG Chapter 7 instructs experienced missionaries to teach grammar/vocabulary to juniors, rehearse lessons and common situations together, and includes an illustrative account of a senior companion transforming a junior's proficiency by consistently assigning him teaching parts. This gives every learner both a permanent interlocutor and a permanent audience with stakes — the single structural feature a solo learner most needs to engineer a substitute for.
Sources: https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/manual/preach-my-gospel-a-guide-to-missionary-service/how-can-i-better-learn-my-mission-language?lang=eng

### Nearly all research on MTC/missionary language learning is BYU-affiliated and observational; there are no controlled comparisons of MTC methods against alternative curricula, and internal effectiveness claims (e.g., TALL's pronunciation superiority) are unpublished.
The Hansen (2012) volume, the BYU theses, and Clifford's data all come from researchers inside the community, studying a self-selected population, with outcome measurement at end-of-mission rather than end-of-MTC. This does not make the findings wrong — the OPI data is externally standardized and the methods converge with mainstream SLA evidence on task-based learning, comprehensible output, and self-regulation — but claims like 'the MTC method produces fluency in 6 weeks' are not supported by any published measurement.
Sources: https://benjamins.com/catalog/sibil.45; https://artshumanitieshawaii.org/assets/hansen,-lynne---the-lds-missionary-experience.pdf; https://journals.linguisticsociety.org/booknotices/?p=2284

## Design implications
- Adopt a written SYL protocol as the contract for every AI session: 100% Spanish, with the MTC's formal escape hatch — the learner must ask '¿Puedo decirlo en inglés?' before switching, the AI supplies the missing Spanish, and every supplied item goes into a 'words I lacked' log dictated at session end. This log is the session's primary artifact and feeds the SRS deck (SYL guideline #4).
- Organize the curriculum around communicative tasks with deliverables, not a grammar sequence: each session is built on something to accomplish (teach a 5-10 minute prepared 'lesson' on any topic, persuade, narrate, negotiate), with the AI role-playing a curious/skeptical listener who interrupts with questions — a direct analog of the MTC's Training Resource Center role-plays and Leaver's task-based framing. Grammar is studied the MTC/PMG way: in service of tomorrow's specific task.
- Use teach-to-learn deliberately: preparing and delivering explanations is the MTC's core language engine. Rotating recurring 'personas' (the AI plays the same fictional person across sessions, with persona state saved externally and pasted in) replicates the MTC's 'progressing investigator' — progressively harder questions from a familiar character.
- Engineer the external memory system to play the roles of companion, teacher, and TALL: a curriculum-state document (current goals, error log, scripts in rotation, SRS-due items, persona states, self-ratings) pasted or read at the start of every fresh session, and an end-of-session AI-generated debrief (errors heard, new vocabulary, goal outcomes) appended to it. TALL's core virtue — 'systematic reviews the software never forgets' — must live in this document plus Anki, since GPT-Live-1 remembers nothing.
- Replicate the daily plan-study-teach accountability cycle: open each session by stating the day's goals aloud in Spanish, close with a spoken self-evaluation against those goals, and do a weekly written evaluation of the whole study plan (PMG Chapter 7's weekly review with 'companion and leaders' — here, the AI plus the log).
- Use memorized functional scripts the way the post-2004 MTC does, not the pre-2004 way: memorize a small set of high-frequency personal scripts early (self-introduction, your history with Spanish, opinions, past-tense narration frames) to buy fluency under pressure, then systematically vary and personalize them — and heed PMG's explicit warning against hiding behind lengthy memorized material.
- Split the 500 hours MTC-style into interactive sessions plus a separate daily drill block: the AI conversation hour is the classroom/TRC; a 20-40 minute TALL-analog block (Anki sentence cards mined from session transcripts, audio shadowing, record-and-compare pronunciation against native audio) does the spaced review and pronunciation work the conversation hour won't. The Betteridge finding applies to the learner's tools too: proactivity, explicit goals for each tool session, and heavy use of feedback features predict CALL success.
- Teach yourself the rating criteria and test cheaply but regularly: learn the ACTFL speaking level descriptors and periodically self-rate recorded sessions (Radford 2014: rubric knowledge alone improved MTC speaking proficiency); use elicited imitation (AI reads progressively longer sentences to repeat verbatim) as a quick internal proficiency probe (Thompson 2013 built exactly this for the MTC); and buy 2-3 official OPIc or similar assessments across the 500 hours as external ground truth for grammar and accent claims the AI cannot reliably referee.
- Budget real-human hours: the strongest predictor in the missionary data is interactive time with native speakers, and AI conversation is an untested substitute. Even 10-15% of total hours with human tutors/exchange partners (italki etc.) mirrors the 'native companion' effect and provides honest evaluation of intelligibility and accent.
- Deliberately rotate domains and registers to avoid the 'mission Spanish' failure mode: returned missionaries are strong in their functional domain and weak outside it, with persistent grammar deficits (Clifford: only 6% Superior). Schedule topic rotation (work, health, politics, humor, service encounters) and explicit accuracy remediation from the error log — the accuracy work missionaries mostly never do.
- Phase the program like MTC-then-field: an intensive front-loaded phase (heavier scripting, drills, and scaffolded tasks) followed by a long 'in-field' phase of freer conversation plus mandatory 30-60 minutes of daily structured study — and finish the systematic grammar review within the first ~6 months rather than dribbling it across the whole program (Green's recommendation, matching the First-12-Weeks structure).
- Engineer motivation substitutes consciously, since the MTC's purpose/identity/cohort engine is the least transferable part: tie tasks to a real upcoming purpose (trips to Mexico, specific people to talk to), make weekly written reports to a human, and track hours and level milestones — accountability and purpose, not gamification, are what the MTC actually uses.
- Calibrate expectations with the MTC's own numbers: ~400 immersive hours (the MTC phase) yields task-functional but non-fluent Spanish; B2 took missionaries 4,000-6,000 hours of use. For a rusty AP-4 false-intermediate, 500 well-designed hours plausibly targets solid conversational B2 in speaking/listening (relearning 'savings' effects are large), but claims that any 6-week method produces fluency are unsupported by published measurement.

## Open questions
- What ACTFL/CEFR level do Spanish missionaries actually reach at MTC exit? The MTC measures this internally (MLPT, elicited-imitation tests) but has never published exit-level distributions — the single biggest evidence gap for judging the 6-week method itself.
- How much of the returned-missionary Advanced-level outcome is attributable to MTC methods versus selection (young, motivated, cohort-immersed) and sheer field volume? No controlled or even well-adjusted observational comparison exists.
- Does AI conversation partially substitute for the 'native companion' effect (the strongest measured predictor of oral proficiency), and at what discount rate? No published research yet compares real-time AI voice practice to human interaction at scale.
- What are the exact current (2024-2026) MTC schedules and the daily split between language-specific and doctrinal instruction, given post-2020 home-MTC hybrid changes? Public sources describe the 2011-2016 era best.
- How effective was TALL/Embark measured against alternatives? The claim that TALL 'improves pronunciation better than any other MTC activity' is internal and unpublished; independent evaluation of Embark does not appear to exist.
- How well do the MTC's accountability effects survive when the accountability partner is an AI rather than a human companion who can be disappointed — i.e., does synthetic accountability change behavior the way social accountability does?
- What does the missionary attrition literature imply for scheduling within a 500-hour plan — specifically whether front-loading intensity (MTC-style) beats even distribution for a relearner, given the large documented 'savings' effects in relearning?
